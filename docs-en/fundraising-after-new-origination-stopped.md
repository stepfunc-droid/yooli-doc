# Where Did Dingcunbao Funds Go After Yooli Stopped New Loan Origination?

> Updated: 2026-10-16  
> Chinese original: [`停止新增放款后仍募集定存宝的资金去向问题.md`](../docs/停止新增放款后仍募集定存宝的资金去向问题.md)  
> This article addresses one narrow question that can currently be established from public materials: **Yooli said that it stopped “new loan disbursements” on 2020-06-30, yet as late as November 2020 reporters could still see 3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-month Dingcunbao plans accepting new lender funds.**
>
> Those facts may appear contradictory, but they are not necessarily so. Historical Dingcunbao agreements allowed new lender funds to be used to **acquire existing claims from other lenders**. Therefore, “no longer creating new loans for new borrowers” and “continuing to accept funds to take over existing platform claims” could coexist.
>
> The real question is not the wording inconsistency, but **what specific claims each unit of money entering Dingcunbao after 2020-06-30 was actually matched to.**  
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

---

## 1. 2020-06-30: the platform publicly said it had stopped new loan disbursements

In media interviews on 2020-07-24 and 2020-07-25, Yooli customer service said:

- Yooli had stopped new loan disbursements on **2020-06-30**;
- the platform still had about 1.4 million existing borrowers;
- it was then processing some 3-month Dingcunbao users whose service periods had already ended on **2020-01-01**;
- slower claim transfer was attributed in part to fewer lenders and to COVID-related deterioration in borrowers’ repayment ability;
- if borrowers referred by partner institutions failed to meet contractual conditions, the relevant partners would repurchase or compensate those assets.

Sources:

- Nanfang Metropolis Daily / Nanfang+:  
  https://static.nfnews.com/content/202007/25/c3813683.html
- 21st Century Business Herald republication:  
  https://m.21jingji.com/article/20200726/herald/bfee2ceb2b63eface005a59bd7d2be11.html

Thus, **at least according to Yooli’s public July 2020 wording, new borrower-side loan origination stopped after June 30.**

“Stopping new loan disbursements” is most naturally understood as stopping the creation of new principal obligations for new borrowers, not as stopping all transfers of old claims among lenders.

---

## 2. 2020-11-23: Dingcunbao was still open for new participation

On 2020-11-24, *21st Century Business Herald* published an article on the final phase of the P2P industry. In a test on November 23, the reporter recorded that Yooli still offered:

- **3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-month** Dingcunbao service periods;
- all four remained open for participation;
- fundraising periods ran from **2020-11-20 to 2020-11-23**;
- expected annual returns ranged from **6% to 10%**;
- lenders were still actually participating that day, although in smaller numbers than on some other surviving platforms;
- the report also noted that Yooli at the time **did not disclose outstanding loan balance and number of outstanding loans**.

Source:

- Sina Finance republication of 21st Century Business Herald:  
  https://finance.sina.com.cn/roll/2020-11-24/doc-iiznctke2924480.shtml

This was almost five months after June 30.

It can therefore be established that:

> **After announcing that new loan disbursements had stopped, Yooli did not immediately stop accepting new lender funds. At least through late November 2020, Dingcunbao continued to raise funds under 3–24 month service periods with expected annual returns of 6%–10%.**

---

## 3. This does not by itself prove that Yooli used the new funds to make new loans to new borrowers

The media article described surviving platforms generally as still “issuing new products,” but in Yooli’s case the reporter observed **new Dingcunbao fundraising pages**. The report did not trace those funds through to the first-disbursement date of specific underlying loan contracts.

Yooli’s historical Dingcunbao agreements had long permitted:

- new funds to be lent directly to borrowers;
- new funds to be used to **acquire claims already held by other lenders**;
- exit at the end of the service period through another claim transfer.

The V3 agreement expressly described Dingcunbao automatic lending as including, but not limited to, “lending funds to borrowers” and “using funds to acquire claims.”

Source:

- https://www.yooli.com/static/laws/dcbxyStaticV3.jsp

Accordingly, both of the following could be true at the same time:

```text
A. No new loans were created for new borrowers after 2020-06-30.

B. In November 2020, new lenders were still allowed to use cash to acquire existing Yooli loan claims.
```

If B is what occurred, new funds were effectively supplying **exit liquidity to old lenders**, rather than increasing the platform’s underlying loan balance through fresh borrower origination.

---

## 4. But this raises a more important question: whose old claims did the new money acquire?

By July 2020 Yooli was already publicly reporting:

- about **RMB 2.678 billion** overdue;
- cumulative compensation of about **RMB 4.891 billion**;
- some users whose service periods ended in early January still unable to complete claim transfer in July;
- a declining lender base;
- severe claim-transfer congestion.

Against that background, the key look-through question for November fundraising is:

> **Which underlying claims were assigned to the new funds?**

At minimum, a claim-level review should identify:

1. underlying loan-contract ID;
2. original first-disbursement date;
3. whether the borrower was then making normal payments;
4. whether the new user’s transaction was a “first disbursement” or a “claim acquisition”;
5. identity of the prior claim holder;
6. when the prior lender’s Dingcunbao service period ended;
7. transfer price and whether principal was transferred at par;
8. whether the underlying loan already had historical delinquency, compensation, extension, or collection records;
9. what risk information was shown to the new assignee at the time.

If most new November 2020 funds were acquiring old claims, this is not a peripheral issue. It would be a central component of Yooli’s pre-wind-down liquidity structure.

---

## 5. Why “claim transfer” should not be treated as merely an ordinary secondary market

A normal secondary claim market can be described as:

```text
Lender A wants to exit early
        ↓
Lender B voluntarily acquires a transparent old claim
        ↓
The creditor changes from A to B
```

Yooli’s historical Dingcunbao structure went further:

- the product service period and the underlying loan term were often different;
- users’ routine “maturity exit” itself depended heavily on automatic claim transfer;
- the November 2019 product page simultaneously said the platform did not guarantee transfer timing and advertised that historically funds arrived within one business day after the service period ended;
- once claim transfer congested in 2020, money from new users became one of the important potential sources of liquidity allowing earlier users to cash out.

That naturally creates a negative feedback loop when new participation falls:

```text
new lender funds decline
        ↓
fewer buyers for old claims
        ↓
old lenders exit more slowly
        ↓
market confidence falls further
        ↓
fewer new users enter
        ↓
claim-transfer congestion worsens
```

This mechanism does **not** require an illegal fund pool. It is sufficient to explain why regulatory “three reductions” and collapsing sector confidence could hit Dingcunbao especially hard.

---

## 6. The “three reductions” policy made this liquidity model increasingly difficult to sustain

In July 2019, the relevant internet-finance and online-lending rectification bodies publicly said that the second half of the year would continue to strictly implement reductions in:

- number of institutions;
- industry scale;
- number of participants;
- while transformation and orderly exit became the principal policy direction.

Source:

- Cyberspace Administration of China / Xinhua:  
  https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-07/07/c_1124719562.htm

This was structural pressure on any product that relied on a steady flow of other users willing to acquire old claims in order to provide a fixed-term-like exit experience.

Accordingly, it is not surprising that Yooli first showed increasingly slow claim transfer from late 2019 onward. The more serious later development was that **credit risk also began to pass through at the same time**, further reducing the pool of willing claim buyers.

## 7. There is a privately compiled “December 2019–May 2020 funding bridge” data set, but it should not yet be treated as established fact

A lender using the name Sophia8633 (“Sister So”) published a video on 2020-06-20 containing a set of figures for December 2019 through May 2020. The compilation claimed:

- new lender funds of about **RMB 1.629 billion**;
- new loan disbursements of about **RMB 3.751 billion**;
- outstanding balance fell by only about **RMB 647 million**;
- actual claim transfers were about **RMB 1.628 billion** and declined month by month.

These figures have been cited in lender complaints alleging that borrower repayments continued to be used for further lending.

At present there are two major problems:

1. the original public API or screenshot underlying each monthly figure has not been fully verified;
2. it is unclear whether “new disbursements” included rematching of existing claims, renewals/extensions, or other platform statistical definitions.

Accordingly, this article **does not treat the RMB 3.751 billion figure or the other totals as verified platform facts**, and does not use them to conclude that Yooli withheld repayments or operated a new-money-to-old-money scheme.

Those figures should be upgraded to evidence only if the original monthly tables, interface snapshots, or claim-level ledgers can be recovered.

---

## 8. Most important current conclusion

The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Yooli publicly said that it stopped new loan disbursements on 2020-06-30, but Dingcunbao was still accepting new lender funds on 2020-11-23. Those facts are not necessarily contradictory because the Dingcunbao contract allowed acquisition of existing claims. They do, however, make the ultimate asset matching of post-June-30 funds a question that must be investigated at claim level.**

> **If most of those funds were used to acquire old claims, then new lender money entering the platform in the second half of 2020 may have continued to provide liquidity for earlier lenders’ cash exits. Whether this structure existed and at what scale cannot be inferred reliably from aggregate totals; it requires actual claim-transfer records.**

---

## 9. Public web searches are unlikely to move this question much further

The evidence that would actually answer it is:

- Dingcunbao fundraising ledgers for each product from July through November 2020;
- claim IDs matched to each incoming contribution;
- date on which each claim was first originated;
- the assignment chain of each claim;
- bank-depository settlement records between prior and new lenders;
- lists of borrower-side contracts newly created after 2020-06-30;
- claim-level data Yooli submitted to regulators.

Any one of those data sets would make it possible to determine directly where funds raised after new origination stopped actually went.
