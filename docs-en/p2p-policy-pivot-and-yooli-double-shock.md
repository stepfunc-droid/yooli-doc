# China's P2P Policy Pivot and the “Double Shock” Mechanism at Yooli

> Compiled: 2026-10-17  
> Chinese original: [`P2P政策转向与有利网二次打击机制分析.md`](../docs/P2P政策转向与有利网二次打击机制分析.md)  
> The term **“double shock”** used in this article is not an official regulatory term. It is an analytical framework for explaining Yooli's 2018–2020 risk evolution: **first, P2P policy gradually shifted from “continue operating after rectification and filing” toward “balance reduction, transformation, and exit,” directly weakening platforms' room to expand and maintain claim-transfer liquidity; second, the wave of platform failures and expected exits also weakened repayment discipline among some borrowers, producing the regulator-recognized phenomenon of “malicious debt evasion by taking advantage of platform distress,” thereby adding pressure on the asset-recovery side.**
>
> **Important evidentiary boundary:** the public record establishes that China's P2P regulatory direction changed materially, that regulators recognized malicious debt evasion during platform distress as an industry problem, and that Yooli simultaneously experienced declining claim-transfer liquidity, weaker borrower collections, and sharp increases in compensation and overdue amounts. Public evidence **cannot quantify how much of Yooli's bad debt was newly caused by policy change or malicious debt evasion**, and Yooli's later bad debts cannot all be attributed to external policy. In fact, the serious-default-loan data show that many later bad loans had reached their original contractual maturity before the exit policy became fully dominant.
>
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

## 1. Conclusion first

The public record best supports the following evolution:

1. **In 2015–2016, national P2P policy still contemplated healthy development after regulation and rectification.** The 2015 multi-ministry Guiding Opinions called for “encouraging innovation, preventing risks, promoting benefits and avoiding harm, and achieving healthy development.” The 2016 Interim Measures formally defined P2P platforms as online-lending information intermediaries and established a filing-registration system.
2. **As late as early 2018, the industry still treated “complete rectification and obtain filing” as the survival path for continued operation.** Rectification-and-acceptance arrangements issued in late 2017 had at one point aimed for major platforms to complete filing work in the first half of 2018. Leading platforms therefore continued investing in bank depository, information disclosure, audits, cybersecurity grading, capital increases, and compliance rectification.
3. **After the wave of P2P failures in mid-2018, the policy focus moved visibly from “survive after rectification” toward risk disposal, scale reduction, and orderly exit.** National risk-response measures in August 2018 simultaneously emphasized regulating platform exits, enforcing responsibilities of platforms and shareholders, punishing malicious exits, and punishing borrowers who maliciously evaded debts.
4. **From late 2018 through 2019 that direction became clearer.** The document widely known as “Document No. 175” made institutional exit a principal work direction. In July 2019, the two national rectification offices publicly said the next stage should center on “transformation and orderly exit,” guiding the large majority of institutions toward voluntary liquidation, cessation, exit, or transformation.
5. **By November 2019, the remaining path for a small number of institutions was mainly conversion into licensed microloan companies rather than indefinite operation under the original P2P identity.** By mid-November 2020, the number of actually operating P2P institutions nationwide had fallen to zero.
6. For Yooli, this shift plausibly created a first shock: **continued reductions in lenders and new business reduced buyers in the claim-transfer market on which Dingcunbao relied; liquidity buffers based on ongoing platform activity, claim transfers, and guarantee compensation became harder to maintain.**
7. A second shock came from the asset side: **regulators had already confirmed in 2018 that some borrowers were taking advantage of P2P distress to maliciously evade debt and began incorporating relevant information into the credit-reporting system.** When the market came to expect that the entire sector was exiting, some borrowers' repayment incentives, expectations about future collection, and the practical effectiveness of collection could deteriorate.
8. **Yooli's late-2019 to 2020 data are highly consistent with this two-sided squeeze.** Claim transfers slowed first; borrower runoff then stalled, overdue amounts rose sharply, and cumulative compensation jumped from about RMB 603 million to about RMB 4.891 billion.
9. Policy is therefore better understood as a **risk amplifier and catalyst for risk transmission**, not the origin of all Yooli problems. Later serious-default notices show that a large amount of the worst-performing loan stock had already reached original contractual maturity before October 2019, and some by end-2018, indicating underlying credit problems predated the final exit phase of P2P policy.

The central analytical conclusion is:

> **Yooli's crisis is more plausibly explained by the combined effect of pre-existing asset-quality problems, long-term buffering through guarantee compensation and claim transfer, contraction of business and liquidity caused by the P2P policy pivot, and industry distress that worsened strategic debt evasion and collection efficiency. The policy pivot may not have created the original bad loans, but it likely hit both the liability/liquidity side and the asset/recovery side, causing risks that had previously been delayed, compensated, or rolled over to pass through more visibly.**

## 2. P2P policy did not begin as an “all platforms must exit” policy

A common oversimplification is that Chinese regulation always intended to eliminate all P2P platforms. The documentary record does not support that.

### 2.1 2015: the official tone was still “encourage innovation and healthy development”

In July 2015, ten ministries led by the People's Bank of China issued the *Guiding Opinions on Promoting the Healthy Development of Internet Finance*. The document described internet finance as a financial innovation that should be regulated and developed, and called for:

- “encouraging innovation, preventing risks, promoting benefits and avoiding harm, and achieving healthy development”;
- supporting the steady development of internet finance;
- recognizing P2P as direct lending between individuals through internet platforms;
- requiring platforms to remain information intermediaries and prohibiting illegal fund-raising and platform credit enhancement.

Official text:

https://www.mof.gov.cn/zhengwuxinxi/zhengcefabu/201507/t20150720_1332370.htm

### 2.2 2016: a formal regulatory and filing framework was created

In August 2016, the *Interim Measures for the Administration of the Business Activities of Online Lending Information Intermediaries* were issued. They prohibited self-financing, fund pools, principal-and-interest guarantees, and other activities, but also established local regulatory filing and stated a legislative purpose of promoting the healthy development of the online-lending industry.

The institutional logic was still broadly:

```text
Unregulated rapid growth
    ↓
Set prohibited-business boundaries
    ↓
Rectification, filing, bank depository, disclosure
    ↓
Compliant institutions continue operating
```

Official text:

https://www.cac.gov.cn/2016-08/25/c_1119451974.htm

The 2016 special-rectification plan likewise distinguished compliant, rectification, and shutdown categories: compliant institutions could continue normalized operation, rectification institutions were given deadlines to correct problems, and institutions with serious violations were to be closed or handled according to law.

Official text:

https://www.cac.gov.cn/2016-10/14/c_1119720088.htm

Thus, at this stage policy was not “every P2P platform must eventually disappear.”

## 3. 2018 was the key year when both policy and market expectations changed

### 3.1 Early 2018: the industry was still investing toward filing

Rectification-acceptance arrangements issued in December 2017 had at one point aimed for major online-lending institutions to complete rectification acceptance and filing during the first half of 2018. A February 2018 Xinhua report still described filing as a critical gate determining whether a platform could continue operating.

Report:

https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2018-02/04/c_1122366494.htm

That explains why leading platforms continued investing in bank depository, information disclosure, external audit, cybersecurity grading, capital adjustments, and other compliance programs: **the market still expected that some platforms would complete filing and survive.**

This is also important background for Yooli's later capital increases and continued emphasis on compliance, filing, and regulatory connectivity.

### 3.2 June–July 2018: the failure wave changed the risk assessment

In mid-2018, many P2P platforms experienced overdue loans, liquidation, loss of contact, or sudden shutdown. A July 2018 Xinhua report said that “in the past two months, project overdue and platform exits have increased in the P2P industry.”

More importantly, the National Internet Finance Association publicly identified two simultaneous problems:

- institutions planning to exit should formulate wind-down plans and improve transparency throughout the process;
- enforcement against borrowers who maliciously evade debt should be strengthened so that valid contracts remain effective.

Xinhua:

https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2018-07/17/c_1123139816.htm

This reflects a feedback mechanism regulators had begun to recognize:

> **platform distress not only harms lenders; it can also cause some borrowers to believe that “the platform is collapsing, so the debt no longer needs to be repaid,” further damaging asset recovery.**

### 3.3 August 2018: national measures targeted both “malicious exits” and “malicious debt evasion”

In August 2018, the two national rectification offices held a meeting on P2P risk disposal and standardized development. The ten measures reported by *People's Daily* included:

- multiple measures to mitigate risk;
- enforcing platform and shareholder responsibilities;
- regulating platform exits;
- severe action against malicious platform exits;
- **stronger action against malicious debt evasion by borrowers**;
- prohibition of new P2P institutions.

Report:

http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2018/0813/c1008-30224420.html

The focus had clearly widened from “how rectified platforms continue operating” to **how platforms exit and how claims are preserved during exit.**

## 4. “Borrowers taking advantage of platform distress to evade debt” was a regulator-confirmed industry problem

This is the main factual basis for the second layer of the “double shock” framework.

### 4.1 August 2018: regulators specifically requested reporting of debt-evading borrowers

In August 2018, the Internet Finance Risk Special Rectification Office issued a notice requesting P2P borrower debt-evasion information. Public reporting described the target as borrowers **“maliciously evading debts by taking advantage of the current risk events.”**

Xinhua republication:

https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2018-09/03/c_1123368693.htm

Within a short period, local submissions reportedly reached the million-record scale. That should not be interpreted as one million borrowers all finally adjudicated to have maliciously evaded debt; screening and verification were still needed. But it demonstrates that borrower nonpayment after platform distress had become a nationwide issue requiring a dedicated response.

### 4.2 From October 2018, malicious P2P debt-evasion information began entering the PBOC credit system

The People's Bank of China later stated in a reply to a CPPCC proposal that:

> **from October 2018, information on P2P borrowers who maliciously evaded debts began to be incorporated into the PBOC credit-reporting system.**

The purpose was to strengthen sanctions for dishonesty and press borrowers to repay.

PBOC document:

https://www.pbc.gov.cn/zhengwugongkai/4081330/4081344/4081419/4081727/2025080819055046540/2020022814241631422.pdf

This means discussion of post-crisis borrower moral hazard is not merely an excuse offered by platforms. Regulators themselves recognized the mechanism and attempted to repair it.

### 4.3 But the existence of debt evasion does not mean every overdue loan was malicious

A strict distinction is required among:

- real inability to pay caused by unemployment, business failure, or income loss;
- COVID-era repayment difficulties;
- lower recovery rates caused by weakened platform collection capacity;
- strategic default by borrowers exploiting platform distress or reduced enforcement expectations.

Public evidence does not permit claim-by-claim separation of these factors.

This article therefore supports only the narrower point: **malicious debt evasion was a real industry factor recognized and targeted by regulators; it cannot be used to classify all Yooli overdue loans as malicious nonpayment.**

## 5. From late 2018 through 2019: policy moved further from filing survival toward exit as the principal direction

### 5.1 “Document No. 175”: institutional exit became a principal work direction

In December 2018, the two national rectification offices issued the *Opinions on Classification-Based Disposal and Risk Prevention for Online-Lending Institutions*, widely known in the industry as “Document No. 175.” Public reporting summarized the central direction as:

> except for a limited number of strictly compliant operating institutions, other institutions should “exit wherever possible and close wherever required,” with institutional exit as a principal direction.

People's Daily Online:

http://money.people.com.cn/BIG5/n1/2019/0123/c42877-30586507.html

The document also required operating institutions to control existing scale and investor numbers and implement reduction requirements.

### 5.2 2019 introduced broader “three reductions”

Rectification work in 2019 used “three reductions” at both regional and institution levels, including reduction in the number of platforms, industry/business scale, and participant counts; at the individual-platform level, investor numbers, business scale, and borrower counts were also to decline.

Economic Daily-related report:

https://www.chinacoop.gov.cn/HTML/2019/02/15/148917.html

For a P2P platform whose liquidity depended in part on ongoing matching and claim transfers, that mattered greatly: **even without new credit losses, it became increasingly difficult to solve existing liquidity problems by expanding business or adding new lenders.**

### 5.3 April 2019 still left a possible filing path for a small number of institutions—the shift was gradual, not overnight

Policy did not flip from “continued operation” to “all exit” on a single date. In April 2019 the two rectification offices still called for:

- guiding transformation or orderly exit;
- continuing filing preparation for institutions that were basically qualified after compliance inspection;
- continued “three reductions” and resolution of existing risk.

CAC republication of Xinhua:

https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-04/04/c_1124325745.htm

Thus a market expectation remained that some leading platforms might enter a regulatory pilot. This helps explain why Yooli and other platforms still promoted compliance, filing, and pilot prospects during 2019.

### 5.4 July 2019: the official direction became “transformation and orderly exit”

By July 2019, the national rectification meeting stated clearly that the next stage would:

- take **transformation and orderly exit as the principal work direction**;
- guide the great majority of institutions toward voluntary liquidation, cessation and exit, or transformation;
- continue strict reductions in institution count, industry scale, and participants;
- require institutions planning transformation or liquidation to formulate payout plans quickly.

CAC / Xinhua:

https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-07/07/c_1124719562.htm

By this stage, industry expectations were fundamentally different from those of 2015–2016.

## 6. By late 2019, the continuing path was mainly transformation; the original P2P sector eventually reached zero

### 6.1 November 2019: conversion to microloan companies provided a path for a small number of institutions

The two rectification offices issued the *Guidance on Piloting the Transformation of Online Lending Information Intermediaries into Microloan Companies* in November 2019. The objective was no longer continued expansion under the P2P identity, but resolution of existing risk through transformation:

- legacy P2P exposure was to be reduced to zero during transformation;
- a nationwide-operating microloan company required registered capital of at least **RMB 1 billion**;
- initial paid-in monetary capital had to be at least **RMB 500 million** and no less than one-tenth of the former P2P outstanding loan balance;
- institutions, actual controllers, and major shareholders had to make corresponding commitments concerning transformation and existing risk.

Official text:

https://fjjrb.gov.cn/zcfg/gzbwgfxwj/202008/t20200813_5353548.htm

Thus, by late 2019, an institution wanting to continue online credit business generally needed to **leave the original P2P structure, add capital, and operate through a newly licensed form.**

### 6.2 November 2020: actually operating P2P institutions reached zero

CBIRC chairman Guo Shuqing stated publicly in December 2020 that by mid-November 2020 the number of actually operating P2P online-lending institutions nationwide had fallen to zero.

Speech republication:

https://www.safe.gov.cn/big5/big5/www.safe.gov.cn/shanghai/2020/1210/1423.html

The policy trajectory can therefore be summarized as:

```text
Regulated development and filing expectation
        ↓
Strict rectification and control of new business
        ↓
“Three reductions” and reduction of existing stock
        ↓
Transformation / orderly exit becomes the main direction
        ↓
Actually operating P2P institutions reach zero
```

## 7. First shock: growth and claim-transfer liquidity were compressed at the same time

For Yooli, the first impact was not merely that “regulators stopped it from doing business.” The policy shift hit the operating conditions of Dingcunbao and platform liquidity.

### 7.1 Dingcunbao exits depended on the claim-transfer market

Yooli's normal-operation Dingcunbao page from November 2019 said:

- the service period ending did not mean the underlying loan had matured;
- if unmatured loans remained, the platform would initiate claim transfers;
- transfer completion depended on the claim-transfer market;
- the platform did not guarantee the timing;
- the same page also advertised that historically funds usually arrived within one business day after the service period ended.

Dingcunbao's long-standing “quick exit at maturity” experience therefore depended on a continuing supply of claim assignees.

See:

- [Dingcunbao Claim-Transfer Liquidity and Yooli's 2020 Risk-Transmission Mechanism](./dingcunbao-transfer-liquidity-and-2020-risk-transmission.md)

### 7.2 “Three reductions” and industry exit naturally reduce claim buyers

As regulators required the number of platforms, business scale, and investor participation to decline, while the market saw repeated liquidations and failures:

```text
Fewer new lenders
    ↓
Fewer people willing to hold P2P claims
    ↓
Old Dingcunbao claims find fewer buyers
    ↓
Queues after service-period end become longer
    ↓
User confidence falls further
    ↓
Claim-transfer buyers fall again
```

This is a classic liquidity negative-feedback loop.

It can cause “unable to exit at service-period end” even without assuming a new fund diversion or new bad debt.

### 7.3 Yooli itself later acknowledged that fewer lenders slowed transfers

In July 2020 media interviews, Yooli customer service explicitly cited:

- a reduction in lending users; and
- weaker borrower repayment capacity during COVID

as reasons for slower claim transfers.

At that time, some Dingcunbao plans whose service periods had ended on **2020-01-01** still had not completed transfer.

This is highly consistent with the mechanism “three reductions + industry exit → less demand in the claim-transfer market.”

See:

- [Key Evidence Chain for Yooli's Risk Transmission from Late 2019 into 2020](./2019-2020-risk-transmission-evidence-chain.md)

## 8. Second shock: expected platform exit could itself damage underlying recovery

If the first problem were only “claims cannot be sold,” then in theory the platform could still wind down gradually if borrowers continued repaying normally.

The more dangerous second layer is that **industry exit can itself worsen underlying collections.**

### 8.1 The borrower's strategic environment changes

Under normal conditions, borrowers face a platform expected to remain in operation:

- a continuing collection team;
- credit-reporting and future-financing consequences;
- continuing guarantor, asset-side, and judicial recovery systems;
- relatively clear costs of nonpayment.

But when borrowers repeatedly observe:

- widespread P2P failures;
- many platforms liquidating or exiting;
- staff contraction;
- guarantors and collection institutions themselves under pressure;
- a general market expectation that the P2P sector will disappear;

some borrowers may reassess the cost of not paying.

The debt does not legally disappear. The issue is that **when expected enforcement and collection weaken, the cost-benefit calculation of strategic default can change.**

Regulators' decision in 2018 to create reporting, credit-reporting, and dishonesty-sanction mechanisms for debt evasion itself shows that this behavior had become a real problem.

### 8.2 Collection capacity can also weaken as platforms contract

The second shock is not limited to intentional borrower evasion. Resolution capacity can also deteriorate because:

- operating revenue declines after new business stops;
- staff must shift from acquisition and underwriting toward wind-down and collection;
- guarantors, asset-side partners, and collectors are exposed to the same industry contraction;
- if guarantors lack capital, compensation slows;
- if the platform's cash flow deteriorates, long-term litigation, collection, and asset-disposal capacity can weaken.

The second shock therefore includes both **strategic debt evasion and a broader decline in asset-management and recovery efficiency caused by sector exit.**

## 9. Why Yooli's data fit the double-shock model

### 9.1 October 2019: underlying risk already existed but was still being absorbed by compensation

As of 2019-10-31, Yooli disclosed:

| Metric | Public data |
| --- | ---: |
| Outstanding loan balance | **about RMB 12.6bn** |
| Current borrowers | **1,658,632** |
| Overdue projects | **0** |
| Overdue amount | **RMB 0** |
| Cumulative compensation | **about RMB 603m** |
| Compensated cases | **86,441** |

Thus, even then, it was no longer accurate to say that underlying borrowers had never defaulted: hundreds of millions of renminbi had already required third-party compensation.

See:

- [Yooli's Last Public Operating Data](./last-public-operating-data.md)

### 9.2 Late 2019 / early 2020: claim-transfer liquidity appears to have weakened first

By July 2020, media interviews showed that some Dingcunbao users whose service periods ended in early January were still queued for transfer.

A plausible transmission sequence is therefore:

> **decline in new lending / claim-assignment demand → transfer congestion → users can no longer exit quickly in the way they had historically expected.**

### 9.3 June 2020: underlying collections then deteriorated visibly

At end-June 2020:

- current borrowers were about **1.379 million**;
- the count fell by only **5,556** in one month, compared with roughly **70,000–80,000** monthly declines in several earlier months;
- overdue amount reached about **RMB 2.678 billion**;
- overdue increased by around **RMB 1.05 billion** from end-May.

At that point, “there are not enough buyers in the claim-transfer market” could no longer explain the entire problem. **Actual borrower repayment and asset recovery were themselves deteriorating sharply.**

### 9.4 The compensation system was also under enormous pressure

Cumulative compensation rose from about **RMB 603 million** at end-October 2019 to about **RMB 4.891 billion** by July 2020—an increase of approximately **RMB 4.29 billion** in less than a year.

At the same time, compensation could no longer isolate all risk from lenders and public overdue reached about **RMB 2.678 billion**.

A more complete model is therefore:

```text
Policy and industry expectations shift toward exit
        ↓
New lending and claim-transfer demand fall
        ↓
Dingcunbao transfer slows; liquidity buffer weakens
        ↓
Platform operations / collection / guarantee network comes under pressure
        ↓
Some borrowers lose repayment capacity + some strategically evade debt
        ↓
Underlying collections deteriorate further
        ↓
Guarantee compensation surges
        ↓
Compensation can no longer cover all default
        ↓
Overdue passes directly through to lenders
        ↓
Confidence declines further, weakening claim-transfer liquidity again
```

That is the “double shock” and subsequent feedback loop described in this article.

## 10. Why Yooli's failure still cannot be attributed simply to policy

An opposite oversimplification would be:

> “Yooli was completely healthy until regulators decided P2P had to exit, and policy destroyed it.”

The evidence does not support that either.

### 10.1 Many later serious-default loans had already completed their original contractual terms before the final exit phase

Maturity analysis of Yooli's first 295 serious-default borrower notice batches found that, among 619,387 records with calculable maturity:

- **481,434 records** had final contractual maturity no later than 2019-10-31;
- their later notice-appearance overdue amounts accumulated to about **RMB 6.26570 billion**;
- **301,479 records, about RMB 2.57891 billion**, had reached final contractual maturity by end-2018.

This does not prove that every loan was already in default on its maturity date and does not exclude extensions, restructuring, or compensation.

It does establish that **a large share of the asset pool that later became severely distressed was not newly originated after the 2020 exit phase.**

See:

- [Yooli Serious-Default Loans: Contract Maturity and Publication-Lag Analysis](./severe-default-loan-maturity-and-publication-lag.md)

### 10.2 Large-scale compensation already existed in 2019

Cumulative compensation exceeded RMB 600 million by October 2019, direct evidence that underlying credit problems already existed.

The evidence-consistent formulation is therefore:

> **The policy pivot did not create Yooli's underlying bad loans from nothing. More plausibly, it simultaneously weakened the buffers previously provided by new business, claim-transfer liquidity, guarantee compensation, and continuing collection—and, against an industry-wide exit background, aggravated borrower repayment and debt-evasion problems.**

---

## 11. COVID was a third overlapping 2020 variable and should not be conflated with policy effects

The first half of 2020 coincided with COVID. Yooli customer service itself attributed part of borrowers' reduced repayment capacity to the pandemic.

At least three external factors could therefore have affected 2020 underlying collections at the same time:

1. **continued P2P policy reduction and sector exit;**
2. **strategic debt evasion and lower collection efficiency after industry distress;**
3. **real income and repayment-capacity shocks to consumer-credit and unsecured-credit borrowers caused by COVID.**

Yooli did not publicly disclose sufficient loan-level migration, first-delinquency, rolling-rate, or collection-recovery data to separate these effects precisely. This article therefore does not assign percentages to each factor.

## 12. Implications for the overall Yooli narrative

Putting policy change back into the case timeline makes the evolution more intelligible than a “sudden collapse” narrative:

```text
2012–2017
Rapid platform expansion; asset-side, guarantee, depository, disclosure and transfer systems built
        ↓
2015–2017
Policy still oriented toward regulated development and continued operation after filing
        ↓
2018
Industry failure wave; regulators begin addressing both platform exit and borrower debt evasion
        ↓
Late 2018–2019
“Three reductions” and exit become increasingly dominant, while some leading institutions still retain filing/transformation expectations
        ↓
Late 2019
Yooli still displays normal products, but claim-transfer liquidity begins materially weakening
        ↓
2020
Fewer claim buyers + COVID + weaker underlying recovery + debt evasion / collection-efficiency problems
        ↓
Guarantee compensation expands rapidly and ultimately cannot absorb all risk
        ↓
Large lender-facing overdue appears
        ↓
New origination stops; stock risk resolution begins
        ↓
2021 onward
Platform exits matching business and enters long-term wind-down and debt disposal
```

This framework avoids two extreme narratives:

- **the later policy-driven exit does not erase the platform's own asset-quality, guarantee-capacity, or disclosure questions;**
- **the real external shock created by policy and sector-wide exit to a platform dependent on continuous claim transfers, long-term collection, and borrower repayment expectations should not be ignored.**

## 13. How far the evidence supports the conclusion

### Confirmed

1. China's P2P policy direction did materially evolve from regulated development / filing survival toward balance reduction, transformation, and exit as the principal direction.
2. After the 2018 failure wave, regulators expressly recognized and targeted malicious borrower debt evasion taking advantage of platform distress.
3. From October 2018, malicious P2P debt-evasion information began entering the PBOC credit system.
4. The 2019 “three reductions” and exit policy objectively reduced new lending and platform business scale.
5. Dingcunbao exits did in fact depend on the claim-transfer market.
6. Yooli later cited fewer lenders as one reason claim transfer slowed, while borrower recovery also deteriorated.
7. Yooli's cumulative compensation and public overdue increased by an order of magnitude during 2019–2020.

### Not confirmed

1. No single specific policy has been proven to have directly caused Yooli's failure.
2. The share of Yooli's 2020 overdue attributable to malicious debt evasion cannot be quantified.
3. Not every borrower who stopped paying can be assumed to have done so because P2P was expected to exit.
4. Policy shock cannot explain all underlying credit risk formed before 2019.
5. None of this removes legal responsibility that may independently belong to the platform, asset-side institutions, guarantors, or shareholders for their own conduct.

The most defensible final formulation is:

> **China's P2P policy pivot was likely an important external catalyst turning Yooli's risk from something buffered by guarantees and claim transfers into something that passed through to lenders. It compressed new business and claim liquidity on one side and, in the environment of sector-wide exit, worsened debt evasion and collection difficulty on the other—creating a “double shock” to platform liquidity and asset recovery. But Yooli's underlying credit risk already existed before that pivot, so policy should be treated as an amplifier rather than the sole source of the crisis.**
