# Yooli “Compliance / Pilot” Promotion and Risk Disclosure

> Compiled: 2026-10-13  
> Chinese original: [`有利网合规试点宣传与风险披露分析.md`](../docs/有利网合规试点宣传与风险披露分析.md)  
> Purpose: reconstruct the public information environment Yooli created between 2017 and 2020 around “compliance,” “pilots,” regulatory-system access, filing prospects, zero overdue, and safe operation, and examine how that environment may have affected existing lenders’ risk judgments.  
> **Important distinction:** this article separates the information-disclosure pilot, product-registration pilot, and real-time data-reporting connection from formal P2P regulatory filing or admission to a regulatory pilot. The former are supported by clear evidence; no regulator document has been located showing that Yooli formally completed P2P filing or was officially admitted to the later regulatory pilot.  
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

---

## 1. Conclusion

The current evidence supports a reasonably clear assessment:

> **During 2018–2019, while sector risk was rising and Yooli’s outstanding stock was being reduced, the platform did not principally communicate to users that it was approaching exit or that risk had already materially deteriorated. Instead, it continued emphasizing that it was a compliant leading platform, actively connected to regulatory and industry self-regulatory systems, likely to benefit from future filing / regulatory-pilot arrangements, strong in risk control and information disclosure, while repeatedly using “zero overdue” or timely repayment as important safety signals.**

This does not by itself establish false disclosure, fraud, a fund pool, or misappropriation. But it is important for understanding why roughly 110,000 lenders ultimately remained rather than exiting earlier.

An ordinary lender during this period could see, among other things:

- Yooli was among the first participants in the National Internet Finance Association of China’s information-disclosure system pilot;
- it later joined the first batch of product-registration participants and real-time data-reporting infrastructure;
- CEO Wu Yiran publicly emphasized that Yooli “consistently adhered to compliant development” and continued contractual repayments during the industry shock;
- Yooli’s “risk education” section carried articles such as “filing countdown” and “P2P will still have broad development space after filing”;
- after regulators announced a “mature one, include one” principle for a future regulatory pilot, a September 2019 Yooli article described that policy as a major positive for platforms “such as Yooli that have consistently adhered to compliant development”;
- the same article emphasized RMB 1 billion registered capital, bank depository, real-time data reporting, and future provision of “safe, authentic and trustworthy” services;
- operating disclosures continued for a long period to show zero overdue projects and zero overdue amount even after cumulative compensation had reached hundreds of millions of yuan;
- an archived “compliant operation / security” page with data through 2020-07-31 still retained strong language including “steady operation,” “small and diversified, effectively reducing risk,” “carefully selecting only high-quality borrowers,” and “ensuring timely repayment.”

The central question is therefore not simply “why did lenders fail to run?” It is:

> **When did Yooli internally know that underlying borrower defaults, compensation pressure, claim-transfer liquidity, and asset quality had materially deteriorated, and did the public combination of compliance, safety, filing/pilot prospects, and zero-overdue messaging continue to reflect those changes fully and promptly?**

## 2. Three different kinds of “pilot” must be distinguished

The statement “Yooli entered a pilot” is easily misunderstood. The evidence refers to at least three different concepts.

### 2.1 2017: first-batch information-disclosure system pilot of the National Internet Finance Association

In June 2017, the National Internet Finance Association of China launched its internet-finance registration and disclosure service platform. Yooli was one of the first ten participating institutions, alongside platforms including Lufax, Jimubox, and Yixin Huimin.

The system required institutional and operating disclosures, including metrics such as overdue rates, outstanding balance, and numbers of lenders and borrowers.

This was a real and documented **pilot**, but its nature was:

> **an information-disclosure pilot run through an industry self-regulatory organization.**

It was not local financial-regulator approval of formal P2P filing and did not mean Yooli had been admitted to the later regulatory pilot.

Sources:

- Caixin, 2017-06-05:  
  https://finance.caixin.com/2017-06-05/101098432.html
- Yicai, 2017-06-05:  
  https://www.yicai.com/news/5295579.html

### 2.2 December 2017: first-batch product-registration pilot

When the product-registration function of the association’s registration and disclosure platform launched, Yooli again appeared among the first ten online-lending companies connected to it.

This further strengthened Yooli’s public image as being near the front of sector standardization, but it remained disclosure / registration infrastructure rather than formal P2P filing approval.

Source:

- Sina Finance, 2017-12-08:  
  https://finance.sina.com.cn/money/bank/p2p/2017-12-08/doc-ifypnsip0980735.shtml

### 2.3 2019: the “mature one, include one” regulatory-pilot policy

In July 2019, a national online-lending risk rectification meeting stated that after compliance checks, system access, and data verification were substantially completed, institutions that had basically passed rectification would be included in regulatory pilots according to a principle of **“mature one, include one.”**

That was an official policy direction.

However, no regulator list or approval document has been found formally naming Yooli as an institution admitted to that pilot.

Official source:

- Cyberspace Administration of China / Xinhua, 2019-07-07:  
  https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-07/07/c_1124719562.htm

Yooli’s own September 2019 promotional material then said:

> “This is undoubtedly a major positive for online-lending platforms such as Yooli that have consistently adhered to compliant development.”

The evidentiary significance of that sentence is important: **Yooli did not directly claim it had already been admitted to the regulatory pilot, but it actively linked the regulatory-pilot policy to its own identity as a compliant platform, creating an expectation that it was a likely beneficiary and possible survivor.**

Archived Yooli page:

- 2019-09-04:  
  https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/191.html

## 3. Yooli’s own website did not show that formal P2P filing had been completed

This counter-evidence must be preserved.

The archived Yooli filing-information page listed:

- telecommunications business licensing;
- Huaxia Bank fund depository;
- public-security filing information;
- information-system security classification filing.

But for the actual online-lending regulatory filing, the page still stated:

> local financial regulator for filing: **to be disclosed after filing**  
> filing date: **to be disclosed after filing**  
> filing number: **to be disclosed after filing**

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/recordInfo/index.html

The evidence should therefore not be summarized as “Yooli had passed government P2P filing” or “Yooli had officially entered the regulatory pilot.”

The more accurate statement is:

> **Yooli participated for years in information-disclosure, product-registration, and real-time data-reporting infrastructure associated with regulation and self-regulation, and it continued to promote its compliance efforts and favorable regulatory-pilot prospects. But no evidence has been found that it completed formal local P2P filing or was officially announced by regulators as a regulatory-pilot institution.**

## 4. During 2018–2019, Yooli continued to communicate that compliant leading platforms still had a future

### 4.1 December 2018: CEO said the sector was clearing out, while Yooli continued contractual repayments

In a December 2018 industry review, Yooli founder and CEO Wu Yiran said that:

- 2018 was a process of sector “cleansing and survival of the stronger”;
- Yooli had “always adhered to compliant development”;
- despite the industry shock, lenders continued to receive contractual payments without discount;
- compliant leading platforms would have better development prospects in 2019 after the industry test.

Source:

https://www.cebnet.com.cn/20181228/102543035.html

Such statements had an obvious effect on a user trying to distinguish between platforms likely to disappear and platforms likely to remain.

### 4.2 January 2019: Yooli’s risk-education section published “P2P will still have broad development space after filing”

Archived article:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/121.html

The article’s central theme was that cleanup and filing did not mean P2P would disappear entirely; compliant institutions might continue to develop.

This was a republished risk-education article, not a Yooli promise that it would itself complete filing. But Yooli chose to display the article to its users, making it part of the contemporaneous information environment.

### 4.3 April 2019: the website published a “filing countdown” article

On 2019-04-15, Yooli’s risk-education section published an article titled roughly *Filing Countdown: P2P to Reopen in June; Beijing Administrative Verification to End*.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/128.html

The article discussed the market-circulated conditional filing-pilot proposal, Beijing administrative verification, and expected filing pilots later that year. It also made clear that the filing-pilot document was circulating in the market rather than representing completed formal filing.

Its evidentiary value is not that “Yooli promised it would be filed in June,” but that:

> **At a time when the industry was highly uncertain and users had to decide whether to leave, Yooli’s own website continued to provide content suggesting that filing was approaching and compliant platforms still had a future.**

### 4.4 June 2019: Yooli ranked relatively high in a third-party filing-progress assessment

In a June 2019 filing-progress assessment by 01Caijing / 01 Think Tank, Yooli ranked 11th overall; in another ranking its information-disclosure score was tied for first.

The article itself noted that the filing-pilot plan on which parts of the assessment relied had not been formally published by regulators and could only be used as an observational framework.

Source:

https://www.01caijing.com/article/40767.htm

This was a third-party assessment, not government or platform endorsement. But for users, it further reinforced market expectations that Yooli might belong to the group of compliant survivors.

## 5. The September 2019 promotional article is one of the strongest pieces of evidence

Yooli’s archived “media reports” section preserves a 2019-09-04 article on its cumulative transaction volume exceeding RMB 100 billion.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/191.html

The article concentrated a large number of safety and compliance signals in one place. It emphasized that:

1. cumulative matched transaction volume had exceeded RMB 100 billion as a result of more than 2,000 days of “steady operation” and continuing user trust;
2. Yooli adhered to “small and diversified / real-scenario” lending, “compliant development,” and “comprehensive information disclosure”;
3. after regulators announced the “mature one, include one” regulatory-pilot principle, it said the policy was a major positive for “platforms such as Yooli that have consistently adhered to compliant development”;
4. Yooli had never touched the 13 regulatory red lines since launch;
5. all funds were deposited with Huaxia Bank, described in language suggesting protection of user fund safety “from the source”;
6. registered capital had risen to RMB 1 billion and was described as strengthening “risk resistance”;
7. Yooli was in the first batch connected to the association’s information-disclosure system in 2017;
8. in May 2019 it was among the first 18 platforms connected to the National Internet Emergency Center’s real-time data-reporting system;
9. the article concluded that Yooli would continue compliant development and transparent operation and provide “safe, authentic and trustworthy” products and services.

The importance of this source is that it did not merely say “investment involves risk.” During a period of sector cleanup, it assembled **regulatory-pilot prospects, capital strength, bank depository, real-time regulatory data, information disclosure, risk-control capability, and safety/trustworthiness into one narrative.**

That makes it a core source for analyzing why users continued holding exposure.

## 6. What did Yooli’s long-running “zero overdue” narrative actually mean?

### 6.1 Yooli was already reporting zero overdue in the 2017 first-batch disclosure pilot

When the association’s disclosure platform launched in 2017, media summaries of the first ten participating platforms showed Yooli’s project overdue rate and amount overdue rate at zero.

Examples:

- https://www.hkcd.com.hk/content/2017-06/06/content_1051314.html
- https://business.sohu.com/20170607/n496054509.shtml

“Zero overdue” was therefore not a claim that appeared only in 2019. It had long been part of Yooli’s safety and operating image.

### 6.2 A Yooli help page directly said there had been “not a single overdue case since launch”

A surviving mobile help page states, in substance, that since Yooli launched there had not been a single overdue case and that lenders received principal and returns on the scheduled repayment date.

The same page said that if a borrower became overdue, the guarantee institution would compensate principal, return, and penalty interest within two business days.

Page:

https://m.yooli.com/views/appCommon/help/safety.jsp

Placed together, these statements are important: **underlying borrowers could default, but guarantee / compensation could allow lender-facing statistics and user experience to continue appearing as “no overdue.”**

### 6.3 2019-10-31: website still showed zero overdue, while cumulative compensation was about RMB 603 million

Yooli’s archived operating-information page showed:

- outstanding loan balance about RMB 12.6 billion;
- cumulative compensation RMB 602.75 million, about RMB 603 million;
- cumulative compensation transactions 86,441;
- overdue projects 0;
- overdue amount 0;
- project overdue rate 0%.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/statistic/platform/index.html

This is not necessarily internally inconsistent. Under the regulatory disclosure definition, “overdue amount” focuses on principal and interest that the **lender failed to receive when contractually due**, while cumulative compensation records third-party payment caused by borrower default.

Official rule:

https://www.jsjrb.gov.cn/art/2017/8/30/art_34_25406.html

Thus, if the borrower defaulted but a third party compensated the lender on time, the platform could still report zero lender-facing overdue under that definition.

The more accurate investigative conclusion is:

> **By October 2019, public data already showed that at least roughly RMB 603 million of borrower default had required third-party compensation, while the most intuitive lender-facing overdue metric remained zero. Whether users were adequately informed of underlying defaults, the identity and financial capacity of compensating parties, and the pressure building in that system requires examination of the broader disclosure environment.**

### 6.4 By 2020 the risk finally passed through to lenders

By July 2020 Yooli’s public figures had changed to:

- overdue amount about RMB 2.678 billion;
- project overdue rate 13.60%;
- amount overdue rate 20.22%;
- cumulative compensation about RMB 4.891 billion.

The mechanism that had previously kept borrower defaults from appearing as lender-facing overdue was no longer able to maintain the earlier “zero overdue” result.

The increase in cumulative compensation from about RMB 603 million in October 2019 to about RMB 4.891 billion in July 2020 is one of the most important risk curves in the case.

## 7. Even by July 2020, the website still retained strong “safe operation” language

The archived Yooli “compliant operation / security” page contains data stated as current through **2020-07-31**.

It still used phrases such as:

- “Yooli has operated steadily for more than 2,000 days”;
- “small and diversified, effectively reducing risk”;
- “careful selection, only high-quality borrowers”;
- “multiple safeguards, ensuring timely repayment”;
- plus references to globally known investors, bank depository, and risk-control models.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/security/index.html

The page must be interpreted cautiously. It may have been a fixed page retained from earlier periods; its continued availability does not prove that Yooli newly issued those statements in July 2020.

But it does establish that:

> **When claim transfers were already severely congested and public overdue had reached billions of yuan, a user-facing “compliant operation / security” page still contained substantial language likely to lower risk perception.**

### Additional point: material-events and audit-information pages also lagged behind the visible severity of the crisis

Recovered website snapshots show that Yooli’s dedicated “material events” disclosure page continued to show all seven categories as **“none”** in archived versions dated 2020-09-26, 2020-12-05, and 2021-03-03.

At the same time, the audit-information page through March 2021 continued mainly to provide the **2018 financial audit, special audit, and compliance assessment**; no 2019 or 2020 equivalent entries have been located in the surviving archive.

This adds a second layer beyond merely observing that old safety language remained online. By mid-2020, Yooli had publicly experienced billions in overdue, rapidly rising compensation, prolonged claim-transfer congestion, and cessation of new loan disbursements. Yet the website sections specifically intended for material-events and audit disclosures did not show updates of comparable intensity.

The evidentiary boundary remains important: **the website alone does not prove a legal disclosure violation.** Yooli may have disclosed information through the app, internal messages, customer-service groups, offline communications, or reports to regulators. A legal assessment requires comparison with what regulators knew and what was actually pushed to all lenders.

## 8. Regulatory requirements concerning risk disclosure

The 2017 disclosure guidelines for online-lending information intermediaries required, among other things:

- disclosure to be **true, accurate, complete, and timely**;
- no false records, misleading statements, material omissions, or delayed disclosure;
- disclosure of project risk assessment and possible risk outcomes;
- timely disclosure to lenders when an already-matched but unmatured project encountered circumstances sufficient to affect the borrower’s ability to repay principal and interest on time;
- disclosure within the required period of the cause, status, expected impact, and response measures for material events such as major or complete business suspension, fraud, or events harming lender interests.

Official text:

https://www.jsjrb.gov.cn/art/2017/8/30/art_34_25406.html

The Interim Measures for the Administration of the Business Activities of Online Lending Information Intermediaries also required substantial information concerning borrowers, financing projects, risk assessment, and the use of funds in outstanding projects.

Official text:

https://www.miit.gov.cn/zcfg/xxtxl/art/2016/art_704cdd6af63c4071baf9ac837c5b08fd.html

Accordingly, assessing Yooli’s disclosure cannot stop at asking whether one field called “overdue rate” complied technically with a definition. The broader question is:

> **When ordinary lenders decided whether to continue holding, renew, or refrain from exiting, could they understand promptly from Yooli’s overall disclosures that underlying defaults, compensation pressure, claim-transfer liquidity, and the platform’s ability to continue operating were materially changing?**

## 9. What this contributes to the question of why roughly 110,000 lenders remained

It cannot be established that all remaining lenders stayed because they believed Yooli’s filing or compliance messaging. Individual investment and maturity dates varied.

But continuing to hold exposure cannot be reduced to “lenders knew the platform was dangerous and simply chose not to leave.”

A normal lender during 2018–2019 could simultaneously see:

> the industry was undergoing mass cleanup, but Yooli described itself as a compliant leading platform;  
> Yooli was among the first information-disclosure pilot participants;  
> the platform had bank depository and national real-time data reporting;  
> filing was expected to move forward and regulators were preparing to place basically compliant institutions into a pilot;  
> Yooli publicly described that policy as favorable to platforms such as itself;  
> the website still showed zero overdue;  
> the platform emphasized fund safety, risk control, risk resistance, and “safe, authentic and trustworthy” operation.

In that environment, a user’s belief that “many P2P platforms will disappear, but Yooli may be one of the few compliant platforms that remain” was understandable at the time.

The question “why didn’t the 110,000 leave?” should therefore be split into two separate issues:

1. **Were their claims already objectively unable to exit because of product terms and claim-transfer rules?**
2. **While exit or exposure reduction was still possible, did the platform’s public information allow them to understand the actual deterioration adequately?**

The second issue is the central evidentiary contribution of this article.

The next stage should reconstruct a monthly risk curve for 2018–2020 and place it on the same timeline as contemporaneous promotional messaging:

| Time | Outstanding balance | Current lenders | Overdue amount | Cumulative compensation | Transfer waiting time | Contemporaneous messaging |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| 2018-06 | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | sector cleanup begins |
| 2019-01 | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | “broad development space after filing” |
| 2019-04 | about RMB 14.37 bn | about 209,500 | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | “filing countdown” |
| 2019-09 | to be recovered | to be recovered | 0 / to verify | to be recovered | to be recovered | regulatory pilot described as positive for Yooli; “safe, authentic, trustworthy” |

| 2019-12 | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | to be recovered | visibly slowing | recover app/announcement warnings |

| 2020-06/07 | RMB 11.418 bn | 160,400 | **RMB 2.678bn** | **RMB 4.891bn** | roughly six months behind | website security page still retained strong safety wording |

The most important original evidence would include:

- monthly National Internet Finance Association / Yooli operating-information pages;
- cumulative compensation amount and transaction count;
- identities and actual payment capacity of compensating institutions;
- app claim-transfer queue dates and rule changes;
- risk warnings, SMS, internal messages, and announcements actually sent to users;
- project-level disclosures of deteriorating borrower capacity and overdue events;
- internal asset-quality data known to Yooli from late 2019 through mid-2020.

If the reconstructed sequence ultimately shows:

> **underlying default and compensation pressure rising rapidly → claim-transfer waiting time deteriorating → public messaging still emphasizing compliance, pilot prospects, zero overdue, and safety/trustworthiness → risk passing through to lenders months later**

that would form a more explanatory evidence chain than simply saying that “the platform later failed to complete its wind-down.”

## 11. Evidence strength and usage boundaries

### Grade A: Yooli’s own archived pages

- risk-education pages and articles;
- September 2019 promotional article;
- filing-information page;
- 2019-10-31 operating information;
- security page with data current through 2020-07-31.

### Grade A/B: regulator and industry self-regulatory materials

- 2017 information-disclosure pilot;
- disclosure guidelines;
- 2019 regulatory-pilot policy.

### Grade B: mainstream media and third-party research

- CEO public interviews;
- 01Caijing filing-progress assessment;
- 2020 media reporting on claim transfers, overdue, and compensation.

For litigation, police reporting, or public research, a defensible formulation is:

> **“Yooli long positioned itself as a leading platform actively pursuing compliance and deeply connected to industry regulatory and self-regulatory infrastructure, while continuing to emphasize filing/regulatory-pilot prospects, bank depository, zero overdue, and risk-control capacity. No evidence has been found that Yooli formally completed P2P filing or was officially announced by regulators as a regulatory-pilot institution. At the same time, Yooli had already disclosed about RMB 603 million in cumulative compensation by October 2019 while still reporting zero overdue, followed by a rapid deterioration of both compensation and overdue in 2020. This chronology warrants further examination of whether the platform fully and promptly communicated the substantive change in risk to lenders.”**

That is more accurate, and more difficult to rebut, than writing either “Yooli falsely claimed that it had already completed filing” or “zero overdue necessarily meant fabricated data.”
