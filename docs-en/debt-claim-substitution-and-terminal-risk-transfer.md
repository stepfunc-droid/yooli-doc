# Yooli Debt-Claim Recognition and Terminal Risk Transfer

> Purpose: explain that debt-claim recognition itself is a normal and institutionally grounded tool in P2P wind-downs, while the central question in the Yooli case is **why, after a large group of lenders had reportedly recovered principal and interest in full, the final group of fewer than 110,000 lenders mainly received highly distressed underlying claims and the associated collection risk.**

## 1. Conclusion first

The easiest point to misunderstand should be stated clearly:

> **After a P2P platform exits, identifying, allocating, or assigning underlying claims to lenders so that lenders collect directly or through agents is not inherently abnormal.**

Public reporting on Beijing online-lending exit rules expressly included lender self-collection as one available mechanism. Yooli's locality-based debt-claim recognition scheme announced in December 2021 followed the same general logic: lenders could exchange account-level principal receivable at a **1:1 nominal principal ratio** for claims in selected localities, then collect directly, engage collection services, or wait for continuing recoveries.

The question therefore should not be framed simply as:

> “Why did Yooli refuse to repay from its own money and dump claims onto lenders?”

A more accurate question is:

> **Why did roughly 150,000 existing lenders, according to Yooli's own figures, achieve “full recovery of principal and interest” during the earlier risk-resolution period, while the final group of fewer than 110,000 lenders reached a terminal arrangement centered on recognition and self-collection of a loan pool with extremely high delinquency and bad debt?**

The issue is not whether claim recognition is a legitimate mechanism in principle. It is **who bore risk at different stages, how that risk shifted, and when the platform disclosed the deterioration sufficiently to lenders.**

---

## 2. Claim recognition is a normal P2P exit tool

The underlying legal relationship in P2P lending is ordinarily:

> the lender extends credit to a specific borrower, while the platform is positioned as an information intermediary and matching/service provider.

When the platform stops operating the P2P business, the underlying borrower claims do not disappear. Common resolution methods include:

- continued centralized collection by the platform or an entrusted institution;
- transfer or disposal to a third-party asset-management institution;
- look-through identification or rematching of claims into individual lender names;
- direct lender collection or litigation after receipt of contracts and borrower information.

Public reporting on Beijing Internet Finance Association exit rules said that, where lenders voluntarily chose self-collection, the online-lending institution should timely provide contracts and other information and offer assistance and support.

Therefore, criticizing Yooli merely because the last stage allowed lenders to collect their own claims would be analytically incomplete.

## 3. Substance of Yooli's 2021 final scheme

On 2021-12-28, Yooli published what it called its “final debt payout plan,” centered on a **locality-based debt-claim recognition exit scheme**.

Publicly reported terms included:

1. lenders could exchange their account-level principal receivable at a **1:1 nominal principal ratio** for claims in selected localities through a third-party institution;
2. lenders could select claims according to locality, amount, loan purpose, and other criteria;
3. lenders who did not actively select could later be matched by the system under platform rules;
4. after recognition, lenders could continue holding and waiting for repayment, collect directly, or engage collection services;
5. Yooli said it would continue providing collection-related assistance.

In January 2022 Yooli announced that, from 2022-02-01, the prior cash-transfer exit channel would close and the work focus would move to locality-based claim recognition.

References:

- Jinronghu  
  https://test.jinronghu.com/index.php/news/36102.html
- Kanong republication of the January 2022 notice  
  https://daikuan-static.kanongyun.com/article-53139.html

In economic substance, the arrangement shifted from **“platform-assisted cash exit” toward “lender directly holds the underlying claim.”**

### The third-party asset-management company was an actual contractual buyer, not an abstract system role

The January 2022 notice disclosed that the **third-party asset-management company operating the cash-transfer channel had a formal cooperation agreement with Yooli** and had actually used its own funds to purchase Yooli claims. Yooli said the purchased claims had “quality below expectations and poor recoveries,” and that the parties would not renew the agreement at expiry.

This clarifies the role of the third party in the December 2021 structure. It had actually participated in:

- cash purchase of claims;
- acquisition of original account claims;
- substitution within a claim pool;
- transfer of locality-based claims back to lenders;
- in some circumstances, offering a later discounted purchase route.

But public notices still do not disclose the company's **full legal name, shareholders, actual controller, total face value acquired, average discount, payment path, or later recoveries**. Current evidence therefore cannot determine whether it was related to Yooli, the shareholders, executives, or historical asset-side institutions, and cannot establish below-market asset transfer.

## 4. The real issue: “1:1” matched nominal principal, not economic value

The phrase “1:1 exchange at principal receivable” can sound like principal value was fully preserved. Two concepts must be separated:

> **face value of a claim ≠ realistically recoverable economic value of the claim.**

A RMB 1 million performing claim and a RMB 1 million long-overdue claim against a disappeared borrower with no enforceable property do not have the same economic value.

By 2021, Yooli itself disclosed that:

- current outstanding loan balance was about **RMB 8 billion**;
- fewer than **110,000 lenders** remained;
- unsecured credit and consumer loans represented close to **90%** of the underlying pool;
- underlying overdue rate was close to **90%**;
- the platform described bad debt as close to **RMB 7 billion**.

Thus, when “1:1 claim recognition” became the terminal wind-down mechanism, the remaining lenders were not receiving claims from a normally performing pool. They were receiving claims from a **heavily delinquent stock pool with uncertain collection value.**

The 1:1 ratio therefore described nominal face amount, not 100% economic-value recovery.

---

## 5. The “150,000 fully repaid” figure and the final fewer-than-110,000 group must be analyzed together

Yooli's April 2021 public figures stated that, relative to a risk-resolution / stock-reduction starting point of nearly **RMB 21 billion in principal receivable and more than 260,000 existing lenders**:

- approximately **150,000 lenders** had achieved “full recovery of principal and interest”; and
- by end-March 2021, the remaining outstanding loan balance was around **RMB 8 billion**, with fewer than **110,000 lenders** remaining.

The arithmetic is striking:

> **more than 260,000 existing lenders − about 150,000 fully repaid ≈ fewer than 110,000 remaining lenders.**

This strongly supports the interpretation that the 150,000 figure largely referred to the stock population that exited during risk reduction, although public materials do not prove that all three figures use an identical person-counting definition or close perfectly lender by lender.

Even with that evidentiary boundary, the risk-resolution process shows a clear time stratification.

### Stage one: large numbers of lenders could still obtain cash exits

While claim-transfer liquidity, borrower repayments, and compensation mechanisms were still functioning, many users completed exit. Yooli later described approximately 150,000 existing lenders as having recovered principal and interest in full.

### Stage two: claim transfers congested and exit capacity declined

From late 2019, claim-transfer waiting times lengthened. By July 2020, media reports said that the platform was still processing some exit requests for Dingcunbao service periods that had ended on 2020-01-01.

The previous “service period ends → claim transfers → cash exit” mechanism had become seriously congested.

### Stage three: underlying risk became visible

By mid-2020:

- overdue amount was about **RMB 2.678 billion**;
- cumulative compensation was about **RMB 4.891 billion**;
- normal cash exit was difficult;
- the market began showing large discounted-transfer outcomes.

### Stage four: final lenders received underlying claims

By 2021–2022, Yooli progressively stopped the P2P matching business and the original cash-transfer channel, ultimately centering the wind-down on “underlying claim recognition + collection.”

If the three lender-count figures largely describe the same stock population—as appears highly likely—the practical outcomes were markedly different:

> **an earlier group received cash and was counted as having fully recovered principal and interest; the final group mainly received the highly delinquent claims themselves and continued bearing future recovery risk.**

That is one of the principal structural questions in the case.

## 6. This cannot be reduced to “early exit was lucky”

If the platform had clearly told all lenders at the outset:

> “Underlying assets are rapidly deteriorating, compensation pressure is increasing sharply, claim-transfer liquidity may fail, and future resolution may consist primarily of returning highly delinquent claims to lenders,”

then much of the difference between early and late exits could be understood as a normal consequence of investors choosing different times to leave.

But the reconstructed information environment is more complicated.

During the 2018–2019 industry risk-clearing period, Yooli still repeatedly sent positive signals, including:

- presenting itself as a “compliant leading platform”;
- participation in the National Internet Finance Association information-disclosure and product-registration systems;
- real-time regulatory data reporting;
- website content about filing and regulatory-pilot prospects;
- bank depository, registered-capital increases, and “steady operation” messaging;
- long-running public metrics showing “zero overdue projects” and “zero overdue amount”;
- the CEO's end-2018 public statement that, even through the industry shock, Yooli lenders were still repaid according to contract “without any discount.”

By October 2019, public operating data still showed overdue amount at zero even though cumulative guarantee compensation had reached about **RMB 603 million**.

Thus the public-facing picture remained broadly:

> **the industry is eliminating many P2P platforms, but Yooli presents itself as a compliant leading institution undergoing rectification with a realistic possibility of entering the regulatory framework and continuing to operate.**

That context matters when asking why many existing lenders did not voluntarily leave earlier.

## 7. The core questions are when risk formed, when it was disclosed, and who ultimately bore it

The questions most worth answering through regulatory records, litigation, or look-through audit are therefore not simply “was claim recognition lawful?” but:

1. **When did the platform know that underlying delinquencies and defaults were rising materially?**
2. **As cumulative compensation increased in 2018–2019, what underlying borrower-default rate did the platform actually observe internally?**
3. **Why could public operating figures remain at “zero overdue” while compensation had already reached hundreds of millions of renminbi?**
4. **When did claim-transfer liquidity materially deteriorate, and when did management know?**
5. **Did the platform timely and clearly tell all existing lenders that the normal cash-exit mechanism could fail?**
6. **What funded the approximately 150,000 full-recovery exits—ordinary borrower repayment, guarantee compensation, claim transfers, or other sources?**
7. **Did the RMB 8 billion pool left to the final fewer-than-110,000 lenders differ materially in quality, delinquency, and recovery rate from the assets associated with earlier exits?**
8. **Why did the wind-down's final stage return collection risk primarily to underlying claim holders only after risk had become highly visible?**

Without clear answers, the entire wind-down cannot be reduced to:

> “The platform was only an intermediary; borrowers did not repay, so the claims were simply returned to lenders.”

That formulation leaves out a more important investigation issue:

> **whether compensation, claim-transfer liquidity, and positive compliance messaging maintained an appearance of continued safe exit while some existing lenders remained, with the deterioration ultimately concentrated in the final stock population.**

Current evidence supports this as an important line of inquiry but does not by itself establish intentional loss shifting, retention of repayments, a fund pool, or other unlawful conduct. Those issues require bank flows, claim-level lists, collection histories, and internal risk data.

## 8. Why the outcome feels unfair from the lender perspective

The core complaint of the final lender group is not necessarily “I refuse to bear any borrower credit risk.”

The deeper question is:

> **Why, within the same platform and the same multi-year risk-resolution process, could earlier existing lenders receive cash and be counted as having fully recovered principal and interest, while the final group was only later told, after the asset pool had become severely distressed, that these were their underlying claims and that they would bear the collection and bad-debt result?**

If the platform had fully, timely, and clearly disclosed the deteriorating asset quality, the difference might mainly reflect different exit times.

But if material internal deterioration had already occurred while public communications still emphasized zero overdue, compliant-leader status, regulatory connectivity, filing prospects, and steady operation, then the failure of the final lender group to exit earlier cannot simply be characterized as a voluntary choice to accept the later risks.

The key issue is therefore:

> **information symmetry, timely risk disclosure, substantive fairness of exit opportunities, and whether the terminal bad-debt burden became systematically concentrated among the final lenders who continued to trust the platform.**

This should be investigated together with asset-side relationships, compensation funding, claim-transfer mechanisms, and historical operating data.

---

## 9. Evidentiary boundaries

Current evidence establishes that:

- Yooli itself used the figures of roughly 150,000 lenders with “full principal-and-interest recovery” and fewer than 110,000 remaining lenders;
- Yooli later introduced locality-based claim recognition and closed the original cash exit channel;
- claim recognition / lender self-collection has an institutional basis as a P2P wind-down method;
- Yooli's later public figures showed very high underlying delinquency and bad-debt levels;
- early and late lenders experienced materially different exit methods and risk outcomes.

Current evidence does **not**, by itself, prove that:

- the platform intentionally left bad debts to the final lender group;
- earlier full-recovery exits were funded by an unlawful fund pool;
- Yooli intentionally concealed a particular true overdue figure at a specific point in time;
- claim substitution involved deliberate asset-quality cherry-picking or related-party benefit transfer.

Those questions require complete underlying-claim data, asset-pool quality by period, sources of compensation funding, claim-transfer transaction records, and internal risk reports.
