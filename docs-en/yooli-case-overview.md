# Yooli Case Overview

> Compiled: 2026-10-13  
> Chinese original: [`有利网事件说明.md`](../docs/有利网事件说明.md)  
> Source range: the `yooli-action` evidence repository and archived Yooli website, analytical documents already assembled in `yooli-doc`, the 2017/2018 audit and compliance reports, and contemporaneous public media reporting.  
> Purpose: connect the currently dispersed evidence into a coherent case narrative, explaining how Yooli evolved from a leading P2P platform backed by prominent venture capital into a deeply integrated asset-side business, then through industry-wide balance reduction, claim-transfer failure, and long-term wind-down, while identifying the issues that still require regulatory records, judicial investigation, or cash-flow evidence.
>
> **Important evidentiary note: this article strictly distinguishes among confirmed facts, platform statements/public promotion, media or lender allegations, and analytical questions arising from the existing evidence. Issues such as a fund pool, self-financing, retained repayments, or false guarantees are not described as established criminal facts without judicial findings, complete bank flows, or a look-through forensic audit.**

## 1. Executive summary

Compressed into one long development chain, the Yooli case can be described as follows:

> **From 2013 to 2015, Yooli expanded rapidly with investment from prominent institutions including SoftBank China, Morningside, and Hillhouse, while using strong safety messaging such as “100% principal-and-interest protection.”**
>
> **After 2015, Yooli explicitly moved deeper into the asset side. The platform increasingly became integrated with borrower servicing, asset sourcing, guarantees, and post-loan management, with overlap among personnel and institutions in some parts of the system.**
>
> **From 2017 through 2019, while China's P2P industry underwent rectification and risk clearing, Yooli continued communicating positive signals to lenders: compliant leading-platform status, regulatory-system connectivity, filing/pilot prospects, bank depository, zero overdue, and steady operation.**
>
> **At the same time, Yooli's 2018 corporate financial statements already showed negative equity, large other receivables, and significant prepayments that warrant further explanation.**
>
> **By October 2019, Yooli's public operating data still showed zero overdue while cumulative compensation had reached about RMB 603 million. Yet serious-default borrower notices published from 2020 through 2024 later showed that loans with cumulative notice-appearance overdue amounts of about RMB 6.266 billion had original contractual final maturity no later than that date.**
>
> **From late 2019 through mid-2020, claim-transfer liquidity deteriorated sharply, cumulative compensation and overdue rose rapidly, and by July 2020 public overdue had reached about RMB 2.678 billion while cumulative compensation was about RMB 4.891 billion. Large numbers of lenders could no longer exit normally, and deeply discounted transfer transactions began appearing.**
>
> **When Yooli formally exited P2P matching in 2021, it said fewer than 110,000 lenders remained with about RMB 8 billion of outstanding loan balance. The wind-down then shifted toward whole-account transfers, shopping-mall routes, pass-through of underlying recoveries, and ultimately locality-based debt-claim recognition.**

The central research task is not merely to say “the entire P2P industry failed.” Three questions are more important:

1. **When did material risk actually form inside Yooli, and when and how did the platform fully disclose that risk to lenders?**
2. **What were the real control and transaction relationships among the platform, asset-side institutions, borrower-service companies, and guarantors, and did any relationships require look-through disclosure as related-party transactions, self-financing, or circular funding?**
3. **During risk resolution, why did Yooli report roughly 150,000 existing lenders as having achieved “full recovery of principal and interest,” while the final group of roughly 110,000 remained in a highly delinquent, high-bad-debt asset pool? How much of earlier exit cash came from normal borrower repayment, compensation, claim transfers, or other sources?**

## 2. 2012–2015: prominent venture capital and rapid expansion

Yooli's operating entity, Beijing Honghe Boji Financial Information Service Co., Ltd., was established in 2012, and the platform launched in 2013. One distinguishing feature of Yooli's early years was the speed of its financing and the prominence of its investors.

### 2.1 2013: SoftBank China Series A

In November 2013, Yooli announced a Series A investment in the tens-of-millions-of-US-dollars range from SoftBank China Capital. It was among SoftBank China's earlier domestic internet-finance investments.

`yooli-action` preserves contemporaneous Sina Tech reporting and Yooli promotional material:

- Sina Tech: http://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2013-11-15/14588917610.shtml

### 2.2 2014: Morningside Series B exceeding US$50 million

In 2014 Caixin reported that Yooli completed a Series B financing of more than US$50 million, with valuation above RMB 2 billion. The report also described a model in which microloan companies referred assets, the platform matched funding, and partner institutions provided guarantees.

- [Evidence 2-1: Yooli Company Background and Public Commitments](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-2/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_2-1_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E5%85%AC%E5%8F%B8%E8%83%8C%E6%99%AF%E4%B8%8E%E5%85%AC%E5%BC%80%E6%89%BF%E8%AF%BA)

### 2.3 2015: Hillhouse-led Series C and explicit asset-side expansion

In 2015 Yooli completed another financing round of approximately US$46 million led by Hillhouse. Contemporaneous reporting increasingly referred to asset-side expansion, acquisition or investment in lending institutions, and the desire to control high-quality origination teams.

- [Evidence 2-5: Yooli Completes Series C and Explicitly Expands Into the Asset Side](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-2/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_2-5_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E5%AE%8C%E6%88%90%20C%20%E8%BD%AE%E8%9E%8D%E8%B5%84%EF%BC%8C%E5%B9%B6%E6%98%8E%E7%A1%AE%E5%B8%83%E5%B1%80%E8%B5%84%E4%BA%A7%E7%AB%AF%EF%BC%8C%E6%B6%89%E8%87%AA%E8%9E%8D%E9%A3%8E%E9%99%A9)
- Caixin: https://finance.caixin.com/2015-07-26/100832930.html

The three rounds together reached roughly the hundred-million-US-dollar scale. **Investment by prominent venture-capital firms did not constitute a guarantee of lender principal.** But to an ordinary lender, repeated investment by SoftBank China, Morningside, and Hillhouse was a powerful branding and credit signal and later became part of Yooli's own “capital backing” narrative.

## 3. Long-running early promotion of “100% principal-and-interest protection” created a strong repayment expectation

Alongside rapid financing and expansion, Yooli used expressions such as “100% principal-and-interest protection” and “100% free guarantee by major partner institutions” from at least 2013–2014 onward.

The `yooli-action` evidence chain preserves registration emails, marketing emails, homepages, Dingcunbao product pages, and media interviews. Two particularly strong groups of evidence are:

- Yooli's 2014 homepage and Dingcunbao pages directly displaying “100% principal-and-interest protection”;
- a January 2014 Sina Tech republication of a *China Business Times* interview with chairman Ren Yong, describing Yooli as offering financial products with “100% principal-and-interest guarantee” and explaining that microloan companies performed offline credit review and assumed joint guarantee liability.

Evidence:

- [Evidence 1-4: Yooli Website's Long-Running “100% Principal-and-Interest Protection” Promotion](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-1/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE1-4_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E5%AE%98%E7%BD%91%E9%95%BF%E6%9C%9F%E6%8C%81%E7%BB%AD%E5%AE%A3%E4%BC%A0100%E6%9C%AC%E6%81%AF%E4%BF%9D%E9%9A%9C)
- [Evidence 1-5: Chairman Ren Yong Media Interview Promoting “100% Principal-and-Interest Guarantee”](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-1/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE1-5_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E8%91%A3%E4%BA%8B%E9%95%BF%E4%BB%BB%E7%94%A8%E6%8E%A5%E5%8F%97%E5%AA%92%E4%BD%93%E4%B8%93%E8%AE%BF_%E5%AE%A3%E4%BC%A0100%E6%9C%AC%E6%81%AF%E6%8B%85%E4%BF%9D)
- Sina Tech: http://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2014-01-14/10369095260.shtml

These statements predated the later regulatory framework under the *Interim Measures for the Administration of the Business Activities of Online Lending Information Intermediaries*, which formalized the platform's information-intermediary role and prohibited direct or disguised principal-and-interest guarantees by the platform. The later rule should not simply be applied backward to declare the earlier promotion unlawful.

But the historical record does establish that Yooli's early growth was built in an environment of strong expectations that principal and returns were protected. That long-formed user perception is important when examining why lenders continued to trust the platform later.

## 4. 2015–2020: structural tension between “information intermediary” positioning and deep asset-side integration

Yooli publicly described itself as an online-lending information intermediary, but contemporaneous financial reporting shows that its actual strategy went beyond a passive matching website and moved progressively into asset sourcing, loan servicing, and post-loan management.

### 4.1 Publicly stated strategy: “from a pure platform toward the asset side”

A 2015 *Yicai* report said that after Wu Yiran, previously responsible for offline assets, took over management, Yooli moved “vertically, from the previous pure platform toward the asset side,” including business brands such as Yooli Puhui, Yooli Investment, and Yooli Capital.

- [Evidence 2-3: Asset-Side Expansion and Tension with Information-Intermediary Positioning](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-2/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_2-3_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E8%B5%84%E4%BA%A7%E7%AB%AF%E5%B8%83%E5%B1%80%E8%A1%8C%E4%B8%BA%E4%B8%8E%E4%B8%AD%E4%BB%8B%E5%AE%9A%E4%BD%8D%E5%86%B2%E7%AA%81%E8%AF%B4%E6%98%8E)
- Yicai: https://www.yicai.com/news/4683714.html

### 4.2 Core asset-side teams were large

Reports in 2016 described major staff movements at two institutions supplying Yooli with large amounts of assets, involving a workforce of roughly 2,000 and a loan balance of approximately RMB 5 billion. Yooli CEO Wu Yiran publicly responded to questions about asset-side integration and acquisition negotiations.

- [Evidence 2-4: Yooli's Deep Asset-Side Involvement — Analysis of Asset-Side Team Departure](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-2/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_2-4_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E6%B7%B1%E5%BA%A6%E6%B6%89%E8%B6%B3%E8%B5%84%E4%BA%A7%E7%AB%AF%E2%80%94%E2%80%94%E8%B5%84%E4%BA%A7%E7%AB%AF%E5%9B%A2%E9%98%9F%E6%B5%81%E5%A4%B1%E4%BA%8B%E4%BB%B6%E6%8A%A5%E9%81%93%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90)

### 4.3 A 2020 loan agreement shows structural questions around closely connected asset, borrower-service, and guarantee parties

A March 2020 standard loan agreement preserved by `yooli-action` identifies Chengdu Lixin Puhui Enterprise Management Co., Ltd. as borrower-service provider, Shenzhen Guoyin Shengda Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. as guarantor, and Beijing Honghe Boji as platform party. Combined with corporate records, the evidence repository also identifies overlapping positions involving Dai Hongguang across asset-side, borrower-service, and guarantee institutions, as well as Zhang Xingya across platform branches and asset-side institutions.

- [Evidence 4-2: Yooli Loan Agreement and Structural Self-Financing Questions](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-4/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_4-2_%E6%9C%89%E5%88%A9%E7%BD%91%E5%80%9F%E6%AC%BE%E5%8D%8F%E8%AE%AE%E4%B8%8E%E8%87%AA%E8%9E%8D%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90)
- [Evidence 4-3: Dai Hongguang's Multiple Roles](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-4/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_4-3_%E4%BB%A3%E7%BA%A2%E5%85%89%E4%B8%89%E6%96%B9%E8%BA%AB%E4%BB%BD%E9%87%8D%E5%90%88%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90)
- [Evidence 4-4: Zhang Xingya's Dual Roles](https://yooli-action.online/docs/evidence/yooli-4/%E8%AF%81%E6%8D%AE_4-4_%E5%BC%A0%E5%85%B4%E4%BA%9A%E5%8F%8C%E9%87%8D%E8%BA%AB%E4%BB%BD%E8%AF%B4%E6%98%8E)

These materials support the narrower conclusion that **the platform and portions of its asset-side ecosystem were highly integrated and merit look-through investigation**. They do not by themselves prove that all underlying loans were self-financing or that a platform fund pool existed. Those stronger conclusions would require true ownership/control chains, borrower-level ledgers, cash flows, and regulatory or judicial findings.

### 4.4 External channels and internally built / deeply integrated asset channels coexisted

Later listed-company disclosures, major-restructuring documents, and court decisions show that Yooli's asset sourcing did not simply switch from “external partners” to “all self-operated.” Tongsheng Microcredit, Hechuang Weilai / Youfenqi, Hemei Microcredit / Hemei Zhike, and other independent institutions supplied assets and various forms of guarantee or credit enhancement. From 2015 onward, Lixin Kuaijie, Puhui Kuaixin, Litong Auto Loans, and other institutions with deeper Yooli ties became important production nodes.

Court decisions directly establish that Puhui Kuaixin developed and reviewed consumer-loan borrowers and referred them to Yooli; Litong performed customer verification, credit review, financing-plan design, collateral/GPS work, post-loan management, and collection in multiple auto-loan transactions.

Thus Yooli later had meaningful asset-acquisition and loan-management capabilities, while **deep asset-side integration and self-financing remain different concepts.**

## 5. 2018: risk reduction was already underway, while the corporate financial statements contained funding structures requiring further explanation

Yooli's 2021 announcement retrospectively said that at the high point where “risk clearing and stock resolution” began, principal receivable was nearly **RMB 21 billion** and existing lenders exceeded **260,000**. Combined with the industry's 2018 risk-clearing phase and Yooli's subsequent sustained balance decline, large-scale stock reduction had begun by at least 2018.

At the same time, Yooli's corporate financial statements for 2018 contained several notable balances.

### 5.1 Core figures from the 2018 corporate financial statements

Beijing Surong Certified Public Accountants' 2018 audit report showed, as of 2018-12-31:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Cash | **RMB 128,295,234.23** |
| Prepayments | **RMB 28,334,222.75** |
| Other receivables | **RMB 206,111,615.12** |
| Current assets | **about RMB 362.77 million** |
| Total assets | **RMB 371,140,518.01** |
| Accounts payable | **RMB 163,555,275.32** |
| Other payables | **RMB 226,833,160.40** |
| Current liabilities / total liabilities | **RMB 401,651,638.36** |
| Paid-in capital | **RMB 50,000,000** |
| Undistributed profit | **RMB -80,511,120.35** |
| Total equity | **RMB -30,511,120.35** |
| Revenue | **about RMB 311.11 million** |
| Net profit | **about RMB 3.02 million** |

Other receivables plus prepayments totaled approximately **RMB 234.45 million**, roughly 63% of total corporate assets. The public financial statements do not contain enough note detail to identify all major counterparties, causes, and recoverability of these balances.

That creates a legitimate investigative question: why did a P2P operating company whose formal positioning centered on information intermediation carry such large other receivables, prepayments, and other payables on its own balance sheet? Which partners, asset-side institutions, related parties, or operating activities did those balances represent?

The published statements do not establish the nature of the balances and do not permit them to be characterized directly as lender funds. Their counterparties and relationship to the asset side require look-through work.

The audit opinion itself was unmodified; these 2018 figures should also **not** be used to infer what cash or assets the company has today.

### 5.2 Contemporaneous special audit did not identify prohibited fund-pool or self-financing conduct within its scope

The same accounting firm issued a *Special Audit Report on Key Areas of the Online-Lending Institution*. It said that within its sampling and audit procedures it had not identified Yooli as establishing a fund pool, advancing money for customers, financing itself directly or disguisedly, or issuing wealth-management products to raise funds.

Accordingly, deep asset-side integration and large corporate receivable/payable balances cannot presently be characterized as proven fund-pool or self-financing conduct.

But the special audit was a key-area audit based on materials made available by the platform and sampling procedures. It was not a forensic transaction-by-transaction examination of every underlying loan and every cash flow across the whole life of the business.

Both sides of the evidence should therefore remain visible:

> **the 2018 special audit did not identify fund-pool, self-financing, and similar prohibited conduct within its procedures; at the same time, the corporate financial statements and later asset-side relationship evidence leave questions that still merit look-through examination.**

## 6. 2017–2019: continued signals of compliance, regulatory connectivity, and filing prospects during rising industry risk

This is central to understanding why many lenders did not leave earlier.

### 6.1 Yooli genuinely participated in multiple first-batch industry/regulatory infrastructure programs

Confirmed participation includes:

- first-batch participant in the National Internet Finance Association's information-disclosure system in 2017;
- later first-batch access to product registration;
- in May 2019 Yooli publicly said it was among the first 18 P2P platforms connected to the national real-time data-reporting system operated by the National Internet Emergency Center.

These were real forms of “pilot / connectivity,” but **they were not equivalent to formal P2P filing by the local financial regulator and did not establish that Yooli had been formally admitted to the later regulatory pilot.**

Yooli's own archived “filing information” page continued to show:

> local financial regulator for filing: **to be disclosed after filing**  
> filing date: **to be disclosed after filing**  
> filing number: **to be disclosed after filing**

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/recordInfo/index.html

### 6.2 The platform nevertheless linked itself repeatedly to the expectation of a compliant survivor / future regulatory pilot

In January 2019, Yooli's risk-education page published *P2P Will Still Have Broad Development Space After Filing Is Implemented*. In April it published a “filing countdown” article. These were republished educational pieces rather than a formal promise that Yooli itself would obtain filing, but they formed part of the information environment presented to users.

## 7. The key 2019 tension: public overdue was zero while cumulative compensation had already reached RMB 603 million

Yooli's archived operating page for **2019-10-31** disclosed:

- outstanding loan balance about **RMB 12.6 billion**;
- current borrowers about **1.659 million**;
- interest balance about **RMB 2.215 billion**;
- cumulative compensation **RMB 602.75 million**;
- overdue projects and overdue amount both **0**.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/statistic/platform/index.html

Zero overdue did not necessarily mean that underlying borrowers had not defaulted. If a partner compensated promptly after borrower default, applicable reporting definitions could still show lender-facing overdue as zero because lenders received principal and interest on time.

Accordingly, **“zero overdue + RMB 603 million cumulative compensation” is one of the most important points in the evidence chain.** It establishes that by October 2019 Yooli had already experienced substantial compensation events even while an ordinary user looking only at overdue amount could perceive asset quality as extremely safe.

The questions that remain include:

- how many underlying loans and borrower defaults corresponded to the RMB 603 million;
- which institutions funded the compensation;
- what was the actual payment capacity of guarantors and compensation providers;
- whether Yooli separately disclosed metrics that better reflected underlying default and compensation pressure;
- when lenders were clearly told that compensation capacity itself was weakening.

These issues go directly to the timeliness and completeness of risk disclosure.

### Newer evidence: formal guarantor compensation did not always immediately follow first borrower default

Later court decisions add another important layer. In at least some Puhui Kuaixin–Chengxixiang and Puhui Kuaixin–Guoyin Shengda consumer loans, the first missed contractual payment preceded formal guarantor compensation by months or longer.

One loan originated in July 2016 required repayments from August 2016. Honghe Boji did not issue a Compensation Notice to Chengxixiang until **2018-05-08**, and Chengxixiang formally compensated on **2018-05-11**. The judgment expressly noted that Chengxixiang had not made timely payment when the borrower failed to perform. Other samples show delays of roughly nine months, ten months, or longer.

Therefore “underlying borrower defaults → guarantor immediately compensates → lender side remains normal” cannot serve as a universal explanation. The look-through question is: **if lenders continued receiving scheduled principal and interest during periods when borrowers had stopped paying and formal guarantor compensation had not yet occurred, who supplied the interim cash?**

Current public materials do not establish platform advances, a fund pool, or use of new lender money to repay old lenders. Loan-level depository flows, lender cash-flow records, cooperation-deposit ledgers, and guarantor payment records are required.

See:

- [Yooli Guarantee-Compensation Delays and the “Zero Overdue” Funding-Bridge Question](./guarantee-compensation-delay-and-zero-overdue-funding-bridge.md)

### Further look-through: large amounts of later serious-default loans had already reached final contractual maturity by 2019-10-31

Recalculating final contractual maturity for Yooli's first 295 serious-default notice batches produces stronger timing evidence than origination year alone:

- after removing duplicate images within the same notice batch there are **621,387 records**;
- **2,000 records** in batch 214 lack loan-term data and cannot be assigned a final maturity date;
- among the remaining **619,387 calculable records**, **481,434** had final contractual maturity no later than **2019-10-31**, corresponding to about **RMB 6.26570 billion** of later notice-appearance overdue amounts;
- **301,479 records**, about **RMB 2.57891 billion**, had reached final contractual maturity by **2018-12-31**;
- after applying a conservative strict fingerprint based on masked identity + loan start date + term + amount and removing only exact additional cross-batch records, the pre-2019-10-31 matured set remains **441,695 records and about RMB 6.11316 billion**.

This still **does not prove** that all of those loans were already classified as bad debt by Yooli on 2019-10-31, and it does not rule out extensions, restructurings, or third-party compensation. But it eliminates a weak explanation that these later serious-default loans were simply “not yet contractually due in 2019.”

For loans already past original final contractual maturity and later listed as severe defaults, the actual 2019 repayment, compensation, and extension status is highly relevant.

## 8. Late 2019 through mid-2020: risk did not necessarily originate suddenly, but it became suddenly visible to lenders

### 8.1 November 2019: Dingcunbao page disclosed transfer risk while reinforcing a history of rapid exits

The restored normal-operation Dingcunbao page from **2019-11-18** said that if the underlying loan remained unmatured when the service period ended, cash exit required a claim transfer. The page expressly said **the platform did not guarantee transfer time**, and that a lender might continue holding the claim for an extended period if it could not be sold.

Yet the same one-month product page stated: **“Historically, funds were received within one business day after the Dingcunbao service period ended.”** Product descriptions also used phrases such as scheduled automatic transfer and principal recovered at maturity.

Thus Yooli did disclose the possibility of assignment failure and long holding. At the same time, product language and historical experience reinforced an expectation of fast cash exit. Both sides of the evidence matter.

### 8.2 December 2019: public-facing presentation still looked like normal operation

Yooli's **2019-12-09** homepage still displayed “I want to lend,” Dingcunbao products, expected annual returns up to about 10%, bank depository, risk-control messaging, and a claim that withdrawals could arrive within 30 minutes. On **2019-12-12**, the website's media section still published a strategic-cooperation article with JD Logistics about expanding supply-chain finance scenarios.

These pages do not prove what management internally knew about the ultimate crisis scale and do not by themselves establish concealment. They do show that, during the same period some lenders began experiencing slower transfers, the public-facing message still strongly conveyed normal operation, investable products, and business expansion.

Archives:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/
- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/193.html

By July 2020, media reports showed many lenders saying exit problems had begun around December 2019. Yooli customer service explained that service-period expiry did not mean underlying claims matured; cash exit required claim transfer. By late July 2020, Yooli was still processing some users whose service periods had ended on **2020-01-01**.

### 8.3 Early 2020: several asset-side and guarantee nodes changed in rapid succession

Around the period just before risk became public, a series of independently verifiable events occurred:

- **2020-01-14:** Chengxixiang received a renewed financing-guarantee license;
- **2020-02-21:** Guoyin Shengda was approved to reduce registered capital from **RMB 700 million to RMB 300 million**;
- **2020-04-03:** Puhui Kuaixin announced a reduction from **RMB 100 million to RMB 30 million**;
- **2020-04-24:** Shenzhen Honghe Boji Commercial Factoring, directly invested in by Yooli's operating entity, entered liquidation;
- **2020-04-24:** on the same day, Huai'an Intermediate Court handled several Chengxixiang recovery cases, vacated prior effective judgments, dismissed the suits, and transferred the matters to police because **“Chengxixiang may be suspected of a crime”;**
- **May 2020:** 51% of Hemei Zhike was judicially auctioned because of its parent group's debt problems; the listed company later disclosed net assets of about **negative RMB 397 million** at disposal.

These events come from regulatory notices, newspaper announcements, listed-company disclosures, and court decisions. **Temporal proximity does not establish a coordinated plan or direct causation.** There is no evidence that the capital reductions, liquidation, or judicial events were undertaken to avoid Yooli liabilities. But viewed on one timeline, they establish that important parts of the asset and guarantee network were themselves undergoing material changes around the same time Yooli's public risk deteriorated.

> **Why did Yooli still communicate zero overdue, compliance, steady operation, and filing/regulatory prospects in 2019, while within a very short period the public risk metrics deteriorated into billions of renminbi?**

### 8.4 Dingcunbao still accepted new lender funds after new loan origination stopped

A *21st Century Business Herald* test in November 2020 found that on **2020-11-23** Yooli still had 3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-month Dingcunbao plans open for lending, with expected annual returns of approximately **6%–10%**.

This does not necessarily conflict with the 2020-06-30 cessation of new loan origination. Historical Dingcunbao agreements allowed incoming funds to be used either for direct lending or to **acquire claims already held by other lenders**. Current evidence therefore does not support saying that Yooli secretly resumed making new borrower loans.

The proper look-through question is: **did funds entering Dingcunbao after June 30 finance newly originated loans or acquire old claims; if they acquired old claims, who sold them, what was the transfer price, and did those underlying loans already have delinquency, compensation, extension, or collection problems?**

## 10. 2020: from waiting in a transfer queue to deeply discounted exits

In July 2020 Securities Times / China Fund News reported lender feedback that Yooli was offering discounted claim transfers and, in some cases, account purchases at approximately half of the lender's net contributed principal. The report described this as a “50% principal exit plan.”

The exact 50% price came from lender feedback rather than a uniform Yooli official payout ratio and should be recorded as market exit practice / complaint evidence rather than “Yooli formally announced 50% repayment for everyone.”

Source:

https://news.stcn.com/sd/202007/t20200725_2166328.html

By 2021, platform-tracking reports cited user experiences of successful 2020 transfers at around 43%–48% of face value and more recent prices as low as around 30%. Prices differed by account, time, and route and again should not be treated as a single official ratio.

Source:

https://m.sohu.com/a/460002828_382353

One structural point is nevertheless clear:

> **after normal claim-transfer exit broke down, existing lenders increasingly faced a choice between waiting for underlying recoveries and accepting a very large discount to exit quickly.**

If risk-sensitive users disproportionately sold early at discounts while others remained, deteriorating asset and liquidity risk could become increasingly concentrated among the final holders. That “risk concentration” mechanism remains a hypothesis requiring account-level data, transfer pricing, and funding-source records.

## 11. Why the final roughly 110,000 lenders did not exit cannot be reduced to “they voluntarily chose to keep taking risk”

The 2018–2020 record does not show a simple situation in which the platform clearly warned everyone of imminent large losses and 110,000 lenders knowingly chose not to leave.

Instead, for years lenders saw:

- early principal-and-interest protection and partner-guarantee expectations;
- branding from SoftBank China, Morningside, Hillhouse, and other investors;
- bank fund depository;
- first-batch participation in National Internet Finance Association information disclosure and product registration;
- national real-time data-reporting connectivity;
- Yooli describing the “mature one, include one” regulatory-pilot policy as positive for compliant platforms such as itself;
- continuing content about filing and the future of compliant P2P institutions;
- zero overdue amount in October 2019 operating data;
- repeated emphasis on steady operation, strict risk control, safety, authenticity, and trustworthiness.

By the time large numbers of users fully recognized the risk, claim-transfer liquidity had already deteriorated badly. Exit was no longer a freely executable choice without waiting or accepting a major discount.

A more evidence-consistent description is therefore:

> **some users remained while risk was not yet publicly concentrated because they relied on Yooli's long-running safety, compliance, and regulatory-survival signals; when risk became clearly visible from late 2019 onward, normal transfer liquidity had already weakened significantly and many users could exit only after waiting or accepting steep discounts.**

That is why timeliness and completeness of risk disclosure deserve separate analysis from the general proposition that lenders bear investment risk.

## 12. 2021: Yooli formally exits P2P and acknowledges severe deterioration in remaining assets

On 2021-04-01 Yooli published *Recent Update on the Company*.

The platform said:

- principal receivable at the start of risk clearing / stock resolution was nearly **RMB 21 billion**;
- more than **260,000 existing lenders** were involved at that starting point;
- by March 2021, **150,000 lenders** had achieved “full recovery of principal and interest”;
- fewer than **110,000 lenders** remained;
- remaining outstanding loan balance was about **RMB 8 billion**;
- approximately **RMB 5 billion** of compensation had occurred;
- the platform described bad debt as close to **RMB 7 billion**;
- it described the underlying overdue rate as close to **90%**;
- credit and consumer loans made up nearly 90% of underlying assets.

The same announcement formally exited online-lending information-intermediary matching and set out four resolution methods: pass-through of underlying recoveries, whole-account transfers, shopping mall, and package arrangements.

## 13. From late 2021 onward: cash exit gives way further to returning underlying claims to lenders

In late 2021 Yooli introduced a locality-based debt-claim recognition scheme under which lenders could recognize underlying claims in selected regions at a nominal 1:1 ratio to account principal receivable. The 1:1 ratio described claim face amount, not cash repayment of principal at 100 cents on the yuan.

From 2022, the original cash-transfer exit channel was further reduced or closed. The wind-down increasingly resembled:

> **the platform no longer providing centralized cash liquidity, but returning underlying borrower claims and long-term collection risk to lenders.**

This was a sharp contrast with the early user experience of Dingcunbao automatic diversification, service-period claim transfer, and principal-and-interest protection.

### A third-party asset-management company was an actual participant in wind-down claim flows

Later notices establish that the 2021–2022 cash-transfer and locality-based claim-recognition schemes involved a **third-party asset-management company / claim assignee**.

Yooli said the company had a formal cooperation agreement with the platform and had actually deployed funds to purchase claims. The platform later said the purchased claims had quality below expectations and poor recoveries, and the relationship was not renewed. In the locality-based recognition arrangement, the same third party also played roles in acquiring original account claims, substituting other claims at nominal 1:1 principal receivable, and potentially repurchasing claims later at a discount.

Key transaction elements remain undisclosed: **the company's full legal name, shareholders and actual controller, total principal acquired, average discount, payment path, actual recoveries, and whether it was related to Yooli shareholders, executives, or historical asset-side institutions.**

“Third-party AMC” in this project follows Yooli's own generic description and does **not** establish that the counterparty was a licensed national financial AMC or local AMC.

## 14. Shareholder structure and the 2018–2019 governance dispute

The corporate-governance evidence is relevant to the later wind-down because it complicates any assumption that the two core founders remained aligned and could easily coordinate new capital support.

Before the 2019 ownership restructuring, public corporate and court materials show the operating entity approximately held as:

- **Wu Yiran: 86.10%**;
- **Ren Yong: 13.90%**.

A 2018 shareholder-resolution file recorded an increase in the operating entity's registered capital to **RMB 500 million**, with Wu subscribing approximately RMB 430.52 million and Ren approximately RMB 69.48 million. Ren later alleged in litigation that he had not attended the meeting, that the signature was not his, and that he had not authorized anyone to sign for him.

Corporate files dated 2019-06-26 then recorded both founders transferring their interests to **Beijing Honghe Boji Technology Co., Ltd.**, making that company the 100% parent of the operating entity. Ren likewise denied signing or knowing about the relevant transfer documents.

The available public court decisions did **not** decide the authenticity of those signatures. They mainly addressed jurisdiction, withdrawal, or Ren's standing to bring particular corporate-resolution claims. Therefore it would be incorrect to say that a court found forgery.

Two days after the 2019 transfer documents, the operating entity's registered capital increased from RMB 500 million to **RMB 1 billion**, while contemporaneous reporting said paid-in capital remained **RMB 50 million**.

Current public corporate information shows Beijing Honghe Boji Technology Co., Ltd. as the operating entity's 100% owner, with registered capital of **RMB 500 million** and a corporate page showing **RMB 500 million paid in**. Paid-in capital at a holding company is not the same thing as currently available cash and does not by itself create an automatic obligation to repay Yooli lenders.

The safe conclusion is:

> **the two core founder/shareholder camps experienced a significant governance and interest conflict that reached the courts; Ren Yong repeatedly challenged his alleged signatures and shareholder-status changes, while the available public decisions did not resolve signature authenticity on the merits.**

## 15. Overall assessment supported by the current record

The evidence across the two repositories supports several relatively stable conclusions.

### 15.1 Yooli's risk did not suddenly begin in 2020

The platform had already entered large-scale stock reduction by 2018. The combination of zero overdue and approximately RMB 603 million of cumulative compensation in October 2019 shows that underlying credit losses were already being absorbed by the compensation system. 2020 was the period when risk moved rapidly from internal buffers and liquidity management into public overdue, transfer failure, and deeply discounted exits.

### 15.2 The “sudden 2020 failure” was more likely a sudden *visibility* of accumulated risk than risk created from nothing in a few months

From October 2019 to July 2020, cumulative compensation rose from about RMB 603 million to about RMB 4.891 billion while overdue reached RMB 2.678 billion. The size and speed of the change require explanation in terms of asset deterioration, compensation timing and funding, claim-transfer liquidity, COVID, and the broader P2P policy/industry environment.

### 15.3 Yooli communicated many signals that reduced users' risk alertness

The information environment ran from early principal-and-interest protection and well-known venture-capital backing to later bank depository, disclosure pilots, real-time regulatory reporting, filing/regulatory-pilot prospects, zero overdue, and “steady operation.”

Therefore, the final 110,000 lenders' failure to exit earlier cannot simply be reduced to greed or knowingly accepting a fully disclosed risk. Whether risk was disclosed timely, fully, and understandably remains a separate issue.

### 15.4 The platform–asset-side relationship was more complex than a conventional pure matching intermediary

Media reporting confirms that Yooli actively expanded into and integrated the asset side early. Later court evidence confirms material involvement by Puhui Kuaixin, Litong, Lixin-related institutions, guarantors, and other nodes, with some personnel and operational overlap.

That is enough to justify further investigation into related-party transactions, self-financing risk, and independence of underwriting/risk control. It is **not** enough to describe self-financing or a fund pool as judicially established fact.

### 15.5 The 2018 corporate financial statements merit renewed look-through analysis

The operating company's total assets were only about RMB 371 million while liabilities were about RMB 402 million and equity was negative; other receivables plus prepayments were about RMB 234 million with insufficient public note detail. These corporate balance-sheet items are not the same thing as the tens of billions of renminbi of underlying P2P loan balance and should not be conflated with lender funds. Their counterparties and relationship to the asset side nevertheless deserve investigation.

### 15.6 The final wind-down outcome was strongly time-stratified

Under Yooli's 2021 figures, the starting stock involved more than 260,000 lenders; 150,000 were described as having recovered principal and interest in full; fewer than 110,000 remained with about RMB 8 billion of outstanding loan balance. The numbers are close to internal population closure but are not independently audited as a one-to-one identical cohort.

The remaining group faced a pool that Yooli itself described as nearly 90% overdue at the underlying level and close to RMB 7 billion of bad debt. Risk and losses therefore became heavily concentrated in the final lender cohort.

Whether this was entirely a natural consequence of maturity timing, asset quality, and market liquidity, or whether exit priority, fund allocation, and asset selection were unfair, cannot be answered without account-level and cash-flow evidence.

### 15.7 Policy was probably an amplifier, not the sole cause

China's P2P policy moved from filing/regulated-development expectations toward “three reductions,” transformation, and exit. That transition reduced new lenders and claim-transfer demand, while regulators also documented malicious debt evasion by borrowers taking advantage of platform distress.

For a platform relying on claim-transfer liquidity and guarantee/collection networks, the policy pivot plausibly hit both the liquidity side and the asset-recovery side. But older underlying credit problems and compensation already existed before the final exit phase. The evidence does not support either extreme proposition that “policy alone destroyed a healthy platform” or that “external policy had no meaningful effect.”

See:

- [China's P2P Policy Pivot and the “Double Shock” Mechanism at Yooli](./p2p-policy-pivot-and-yooli-double-shock.md)

## 16. Six highest-priority unresolved questions

1. **What were the true monthly underlying delinquency rate, compensation amount, and compensation balance during 2018–2020?** Why could lender-facing overdue remain at zero for so long?
2. **Who funded the increase in cumulative compensation from about RMB 603 million to about RMB 4.891 billion?** Which amounts were paid by guarantors, asset-side partners, platform-related parties, or other sources?
3. **Who were the counterparties behind the 2018 other receivables of roughly RMB 206 million and prepayments of roughly RMB 28 million?** Did large balances involve Yooli asset-side institutions, related companies, or guarantors?
4. **When claim transfers became seriously congested in late 2019, when did management know that the liquidity mechanism was no longer sustainable, and when was that communicated clearly enough to users?**
5. **Who were the buyers in discounted 2020 exits, where did their funds come from, and how were prices determined?** Were related parties involved, were different lenders prioritized differently, or were better/worse assets selectively reallocated?
6. **How should the roughly RMB 13 billion decline from nearly RMB 21 billion principal receivable to about RMB 8 billion outstanding balance be bridged?** It should be decomposed into normal borrower repayment, guarantee compensation, ordinary claim transfers, discounted transfers, shopping-mall / in-kind arrangements, write-offs, and other asset disposal. It must not simply be labeled “RMB 13 billion cash paid by the platform.”

Any significant progress on these six questions requires bank flows, platform general ledgers, claim-transfer records, underlying-loan repayment histories, guarantor compensation flows, and related-party transaction materials.

## 18. Evidentiary boundaries to preserve when using this overview

To make the material usable in complaints, police reports, litigation, petitions, or media communication without overstating the evidence, the following distinctions should be maintained:

- **May be stated directly as fact:** financing events, verbatim public promotion, audited financial-statement figures, website operating data, official notices, and contractual-party relationships preserved in actual loan agreements;
- **Should be attributed to the platform:** 150,000 lenders with full principal-and-interest recovery, nearly RMB 7 billion of bad debt, nearly 90% underlying overdue, and similar 2021 summary figures;
- **Should be attributed to media / lenders:** 2020 “50% exit,” retained repayments, private account acquisitions, and other claims without complete platform-level cash-flow support;
- **Should remain structural questions requiring investigation:** a fund pool, self-financing, related-party transactions, sham guarantees, selective payout, deliberate concealment of risk, and similar allegations.

The evidence assembled so far is sufficient to show that **the Yooli case cannot be reduced to “borrowers later defaulted on a large scale, so P2P lenders simply bear the market risk.” Yooli's early principal-and-interest protection messaging, deep asset-side strategy, later compliance and safety signaling, coexistence of zero overdue with substantial compensation, dramatic jumps in public risk metrics, and the rapid transition from normal exit to heavily discounted wind-down together form a coherent event chain that requires further explanation and investigation.**
