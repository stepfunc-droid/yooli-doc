# Yooli’s “150,000 Fully Repaid” Figure and the Payout Boundary

> Compiled: 2026-10-13  
> Chinese original: [`15万本息全回口径与兑付分界分析.md`](../docs/15万本息全回口径与兑付分界分析.md)  
> Purpose: explain what Yooli meant in April 2021 when it said “150,000 lenders had recovered all principal and interest,” why many long-term lenders still had not achieved full recovery, and whether the platform’s public information environment may have affected lenders’ risk judgments.  
> This English version is a translation for research use. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

## 1. Conclusion

The most reasonable interpretation currently available is:

**The “150,000 fully repaid” figure can fairly clearly rule out two misunderstandings: it did not mean that Yooli made a one-time cash payout to 150,000 people after the 2020 risk event, and it did not mean that only 150,000 users in the platform’s entire history had ever recovered principal and interest. Given Yooli’s simultaneous disclosures of “more than 260,000 at the starting point → 150,000 fully repaid → fewer than 110,000 remaining,” the 150,000 most likely referred mainly to existing lenders who completed exit during the risk-reduction period. Current materials, however, do not prove that the three figures close perfectly on an identical person-counting basis.**

Yooli’s 2021-04-01 announcement stated:

- principal receivable at the start of risk resolution / stock reduction: **nearly RMB 21.0 billion**;
- peak existing lenders: **more than 260,000**;
- by end-March 2021, cumulative lenders with full principal-and-interest recovery: **150,000**;
- remaining outstanding loan balance: **RMB 8.0 billion**;
- remaining lenders: **fewer than 110,000**.

Thus, “150,000” is numerically almost identical to “more than 260,000 minus about 110,000,” supporting the interpretation that it largely reflected the reduction in that stock lender population. But this remains an inference from published figures, not a population identity verified by independent audit. No independent audit or lender-by-lender payout list has been located that verifies how those 150,000 users exited.

More importantly, the decline in outstanding balance and lender count from 2018 through 2021 suggests that **the 150,000 exits likely spanned the entire risk-reduction period, and a significant share probably completed exit before large-scale withdrawal difficulty emerged in December 2019.**

The fact that roughly 110,000 lenders did not exit earlier should not be reduced to “they chose not to run.” During the 2018–2019 risk-cleanup period, Yooli continued to emphasize that it was a compliant leading platform connected to regulatory systems, had filing/pilot prospects, zero overdue, bank depository arrangements, risk resistance, and “safe and trustworthy” operation. Those signals could reasonably have affected users’ judgments about whether Yooli would remain in business.

## 2. The “start of risk resolution / stock reduction” probably was not in 2020

The 2021 announcement did not give an exact date for the “nearly RMB 21.0 billion / more than 260,000 lenders” peak, but public data place it most plausibly around **mid-2018**.

In July 2019, Phoenix WEMONEY cited National Internet Finance Association data showing that from **June 2018 to May 2019**, Yooli’s outstanding balance fell **34.66%**. The report also noted that June 2018 marked the start of the industry’s large-scale failure wave and a period when many platforms’ outstanding balances were still high.

Yooli’s later public figures continued to fall:

| Time | Outstanding loan balance | Current lenders |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| End-2018 | **RMB 15.4 bn** | not provided in that source |
| Apr. 2019 | **RMB 14.37 bn** | **209,510** |
| End-Jun. 2019 | **RMB 13.986 bn** | **205,900** |
| End-Oct. 2019 | **about RMB 12.6 bn** | — |
| Late Jul. 2020 | **RMB 11.418 bn** | **160,400** |
| End-Mar. 2021 | **RMB 8.0 bn** | **fewer than 110,000** |

This shows that **the stock lender population and outstanding balance had already been declining for more than a year before the withdrawal crisis became obvious in December 2019.**

Accordingly, Yooli’s later statement that 150,000 users had “fully recovered principal and interest” should not be presumed to mean that all were “rescued” by the platform during the 2020–2021 crisis. A substantial part likely exited normally through maturity and successful claim transfer at earlier stages.

## 3. The real exit boundary: around December 2019

Multiple media reports in July 2020 quoted Yooli lenders as saying that **obvious difficulty receiving cash after product service periods ended began around December 2019**.

Yooli customer service explained to media that Dingcunbao did not mean the platform directly paid cash when the service period ended. Instead:

1. after the service period ended, exit had to be implemented through **claim transfer**;
2. once transfer became slower, claims were queued by **service-period end date**;
3. by July 2020 the platform was still processing some users whose service periods had ended on **2020-01-01**;
4. among Dingcunbao plans ending on the same day, the platform had said that plans with **longer original terms** would receive priority.

This is consistent with the contract structure. Dingcunbao automatically matched users into underlying loans; when the service period ended, users exited through claim transfer, rather than because all underlying borrowers simultaneously repaid principal on that date.

### Original-page evidence: before the crisis, Yooli simultaneously warned that transfers were not guaranteed and advertised a historical “one business day” cash-return experience

The restored **2019-11-18** Dingcunbao pages show this exit mechanism especially clearly.

For a one-month product, the page stated that:

- if the underlying loan had not matured when the service period ended, a claim transfer would be initiated;
- completion depended on market demand from assignees;
- **the platform did not guarantee the transfer time**;
- if transfer was delayed, the lender might continue holding the underlying claim for a longer period.

But the same page also said:

> **“Historically, funds were received within one business day after the Dingcunbao service period ended.”**

The page also used expressions such as “automatic transfer at the scheduled time” and “principal recovered at maturity.”

Archives:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/
- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/detail/101.html

This is more informative than later customer-service explanations because it reconstructs what users actually saw before the risk event: **the legal/product rules warned of transfer failure, while long-running historical experience and page language reinforced an expectation that cash would arrive shortly after the service period ended.**

Therefore, **whether an account still had outstanding claims immediately before the risk concentrated is an important factor in assessing whether it could fall within Yooli’s “fully repaid” population. But public materials do not permit lender-by-lender verification of the 150,000 names or the exact classification rule.**

A user who registered as early as 2014 but still held Dingcunbao claims maturing in 2020 could be trapped in the later transfer queue. Conversely, a later registrant whose claims had all exited before late 2019 could more plausibly fall into the “fully repaid” cohort.

## 4. Why lenders did not exit earlier cannot be explained only by their own choice

If one looks only at the outcome, a simple story suggests itself: 150,000 people exited in time while the remaining 110,000 chose to keep taking risk.

Once the contemporaneous information environment is restored, that explanation is incomplete.

### 4.1 Yooli repeatedly reinforced an image of a “compliant survivor”

In 2017, Yooli was among the first pilot participants in the National Internet Finance Association’s information-disclosure system and later among the first institutions connected to product registration. In May 2019, Yooli was also among the first platforms connected to the National Internet Emergency Center’s real-time data-reporting system.

None of this meant formal P2P filing approval. But an ordinary user could easily understand these facts as meaning that the platform was at the front of regulatory, self-regulatory, and data-monitoring infrastructure.

### 4.2 At the end of 2018, the CEO still explicitly emphasized “compliant leading platforms”

In December 2018, CEO Wu Yiran publicly stated that Yooli had consistently pursued compliant development, lenders still received contractual repayments through the industry shock, and compliant leading platforms would have better development prospects in 2019.

Source:

https://www.cebnet.com.cn/20181228/102543035.html

### 4.3 Throughout 2019, Yooli’s website continued to communicate that filing still offered a future

Yooli’s “risk education” section published:

- 2019-01-24, *Chinese Academy of Social Sciences: P2P Will Still Have Broad Development Space After Filing Is Implemented*  
  https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/121.html
- 2019-04-15, *Filing Countdown: P2P to Reopen in June; Beijing Administrative Verification to End*  
  https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/128.html

These articles did not amount to a promise that Yooli itself would definitely complete filing. But they consistently communicated a direction: **P2P would not necessarily disappear entirely, and compliant institutions that completed rectification and filing might continue operating.**

### 4.4 In September 2019, Yooli described the official regulatory-pilot policy as positive for itself

The archived Yooli article *Accumulating Small Amounts into Scale: Yooli’s Small-and-Diversified Model Surpasses RMB 100 Billion in Transactions* cited the regulatory principle of “mature one, include one” and stated:

> “This is undoubtedly a major positive for online-lending platforms such as Yooli that have consistently adhered to compliant development.”

The same article emphasized:

- “steady operation”;
- “adherence to compliant development”;
- “comprehensive information disclosure”;
- not touching the 13 regulatory red lines;
- full fund depository with Huaxia Bank;
- increase of registered capital to RMB 1 billion and stronger risk resistance;
- first-batch information-disclosure pilot participation;
- first-batch national real-time data reporting;
- continued provision of “safe, authentic and trustworthy” products and services.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/191.html

A lender’s 2019 belief that “the industry is dangerous, but Yooli may be one of the compliant leading platforms that survives” was therefore not an inexplicable judgment at the time.

### 4.5 Yooli’s own filing page still did not show formal filing approval

The archived filing-information page stated:

- local financial regulator for filing: **to be disclosed after filing**;
- filing date: **to be disclosed after filing**;
- filing number: **to be disclosed after filing**.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/recordInfo/index.html

The promotional environment therefore should not be rewritten as “Yooli had obtained government P2P filing / pilot qualification.” The more accurate conclusion is:

> **Yooli participated in multiple industry-self-regulatory and regulatory data systems and actively connected future regulatory-pilot prospects with its image as a compliant platform, but no evidence has been found that it formally completed P2P filing or was publicly listed by regulators as a regulatory-pilot institution.**

## 5. “Zero overdue” and compensation: the risk users saw may not have been the same as underlying credit risk

By October 2019, Yooli still reported zero overdue, while cumulative guarantee compensation had already reached about **RMB 603 million**.

Under the regulatory disclosure definition, that is not necessarily contradictory: if a borrower defaulted but a third party compensated on time, the lender-facing overdue amount could remain zero.

But the more important point is:

> **By October 2019, at least several hundred million yuan of underlying default had already required third-party compensation, while the ordinary lender’s most intuitive risk indicator still read “zero overdue.”**

If the platform simultaneously continued emphasizing safety, risk control, compliance, and future filing prospects, whether users could adequately understand the real default and compensation pressure behind “zero overdue” becomes a legitimate subject for further investigation.

Official disclosure rule:

https://www.jsjrb.gov.cn/art/2017/8/30/art_34_25406.html

Yooli’s surviving mobile help page also stated:

> “Since Yooli went online, there has not been a single overdue case; lenders received the relevant principal and return on the repayment date.”

The same page explained that when a borrower became overdue, a guarantor would compensate within a specified period.

Page:

https://m.yooli.com/views/appCommon/help/safety.jsp

Again, this shows that “no overdue” in Yooli’s promotional context mainly described **whether lenders ultimately received money on time**, not whether underlying borrowers had never defaulted.

## 6. What happened in 2020?

### 6.1 Claim-transfer liquidity visibly broke down

By July 2020, media reports said Yooli was still processing some projects whose service periods had ended in early January, while users whose plans ended in late January and later remained in “processing” for long periods.

A clear **maturity-date boundary** had formed.

### 6.2 New loan origination stopped on 2020-06-30

Yooli later publicly said it stopped new loan listings / disbursements on **2020-06-30**, and later repayment cash was mainly used for existing claim transfers and exits.

This can be treated as an important point where Yooli moved from “operating while reducing stock” into a run-off phase focused on existing assets.

### 6.3 Overdue and compensation figures rose rapidly

By late July 2020, media quoting Yooli’s figures reported:

- outstanding loan balance: **RMB 11.418 billion**;
- current lenders: **160,400**;
- cumulative compensation: **RMB 4.891 billion**;
- overdue amount: **RMB 2.678 billion**;
- project overdue rate: **13.60%**;
- amount overdue rate: **20.22%**.

The increase from roughly RMB 603 million in cumulative compensation in October 2019 to about RMB 4.891 billion in July 2020 was extremely large. Whatever the precise statistical definitions, this shows a dramatic change in asset quality and compensation pressure between late 2019 and mid-2020.

Media reports also carried lender allegations in 2020 that repayments had been “withheld and used for continued lending.” Those remain **lender complaints and allegations**, not established facts. What the public platform data do establish is the very large increase in cumulative compensation during this period.

### 6.4 The July 2020 website safety page still retained strong safety language

An archived “compliant operation / safety” page states that its data were current through **2020-07-31**, while still displaying:

- “steady operation for more than 2,000 days”;
- “small and diversified, effectively reducing risk”;
- “careful selection, only high-quality borrowers”;
- “multiple safeguards, ensuring repayment on time.”

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/security/index.html

This may have been a fixed page retained from earlier periods and does not prove Yooli newly issued the statements in July 2020. It does show that after public risk exposure, a core safety page still contained language likely to reduce risk perception.

## 7. Why a particular lender may not have been “fully repaid”

The current record is more consistent with a **time- and product-structure boundary during risk reduction** than with random selection of 150,000 lenders for repayment:

- accounts maturing earlier in 2018–2019, while transfer liquidity still existed, often exited normally;
- after December 2019, incoming lender money declined, transfer capacity weakened, and underlying overdue/compensation pressure increased rapidly;
- Dingcunbao products maturing from early 2020 increasingly entered queues;
- by mid-2020 those queues were severely delayed;
- later the platform shifted toward discounted whole-account transfers, shopping-mall routes, package arrangements, look-through underlying claims, and locality-based debt-claim recognition.

An additional point matters: **before some lenders completely lost the ability to exit, they may not yet have understood from public information that the platform was heading toward full wind-down.**

Yooli was still emphasizing compliance, regulatory connections, filing/pilot prospects, zero overdue, and a safety/risk-control image. The remaining 110,000 therefore likely included both:

1. users who were objectively unable to exit because of product terms and transfer congestion;
2. users who, before the risk became acute, still believed Yooli might survive as a compliant platform and therefore did not reduce exposure voluntarily.

Public materials do not permit the relative shares of those groups to be calculated.

A lender could have used Yooli since 2014 and still end up among the remaining 110,000 if the **last outstanding claims had not completed exit before late 2019**.

For an individual account, the most probative evidence is not registration date but:

- each Dingcunbao service-period end date;
- original term (3, 6, 12 months, etc.);
- principal still outstanding in December 2019;
- when each claim entered the transfer queue;
- actual repayment cash flows after 2020;
- whether automatic reinvestment, rematching, discounted transfer, or other exit action occurred;
- what risk warnings, internal messages, and announcements the platform sent to that user at the relevant time.

Placing those events on a timeline usually shows where an account became trapped and what information had been disclosed by then.

## 8. What the “150,000 fully repaid” statement can and cannot establish

### It can establish, or at least support, that:

1. Yooli itself acknowledged a large group of existing lenders who had completely exited during stock reduction;
2. the platform put the number at about 150,000;
3. that number broadly matches the net decline from “more than 260,000” to “fewer than 110,000”;
4. the platform’s outstanding balance did in fact fall substantially from 2018 through 2021;
5. there was a clear time stratification between early exits and later trapped lenders.

### The statement alone cannot establish that:

1. all 150,000 were paid in cash from Yooli’s own funds;
2. all 150,000 exited after the 2020 risk event;
3. all exits were funded only by ordinary borrower repayment rather than compensation, claim transfer, discounted sale, or other mechanisms;
4. there was deliberate selective repayment between the 150,000 who exited and the 110,000 who remained;
5. the roughly RMB 13 billion reduction in outstanding balance equals RMB 13 billion of cash paid by the platform to lenders;
6. the fact that 110,000 did not exit proves they had fully understood and voluntarily accepted all the risks that later emerged.

No independent audit corresponding to the April 2021 figures has been located. In litigation, police reporting, or public writing, the safer formulation is:

> **“In its 2021-04-01 announcement, Yooli stated that approximately 150,000 existing lenders had achieved full recovery of principal and interest since risk reduction began.”**

rather than presenting the figure as independently audited fact.

## 9. The investigative focus has changed

After adding the compliance/pilot-promotion evidence and the zero-overdue/compensation evidence, the central question is no longer only:

> “Why did some lenders exit while the remaining 110,000 did not?”

It should also ask:

> **When did Yooli internally know that underlying defaults, compensation pressure, and claim-transfer liquidity had materially deteriorated? During those changes, did the platform continue using compliance, filing/regulatory-pilot prospects, zero overdue, bank depository, and ‘safe and trustworthy’ messaging in a way that lowered users’ risk assessments? Did the platform timely and fully disclose the substantive risks that could affect exit decisions, as regulatory rules required?**

This does not prejudge the answer and does not by itself establish false disclosure. It is, however, one of the most plausible lines of inquiry for explaining why roughly 110,000 lenders remained.

## 10. Evidence most worth pursuing next

To understand more precisely why some lenders fully recovered while others did not, priorities include:

1. **monthly current-lender count, outstanding loan balance, compensation amount, and overdue amount from June 2018 through June 2020**;
2. historical screenshots showing what claim-transfer date Yooli’s app was processing at different times;
3. any change to Dingcunbao transfer rules around December 2019;
4. monthly figures for new lending, borrower repayment, guarantee compensation, and claim transfer in 2020;
5. the internal statistical definition or list underlying the “150,000 fully repaid” figure;
6. underlying default/delinquency data actually known to Yooli before late 2019;
7. identities and funding sources of compensating parties and when their capacity became insufficient;
8. risk warnings, SMS, app popups, internal messages, and announcements actually sent to lenders during the same period.

If these materials can be reconstructed, the present “time-boundary hypothesis” could be turned into a much more complete cash-flow, risk-disclosure, and lender-exit curve.
