## 1. By 2018, litigation already showed traces of “Honghe Boji → Lixin Kuaijie” claim assignment

> **Clarification:** the shorthand “Honghe Boji → Lixin Kuaijie” in this heading describes only the surface structure reflected in the litigation. It **does not establish that Honghe Boji was the original creditor or that it disposed of lender claims in its own name.** The public record also supports another possibility: Honghe Boji, acting under lender authorization, helped process an assignment, while Lixin acquired the lenders' claims or a recovery claim created after compensation. Section 5 narrows the interpretation further.

A jurisdiction ruling of Beijing Haidian District People's Court, **(2018) Jing 0108 Min Chu No. 21610**, is an important clue.

The plaintiff was **Shenzhen Lixin Kuaijie Financial Services Co., Ltd.** Defendants included Wei Na, Harbin Jinshengyuan Investment Consulting Co., Ltd., and Harbin Shengyuan Microcredit Co., Ltd.

The ruling records the defendants' jurisdiction objection as including the following assertions:

- nonparty **Beijing Honghe Boji Information Technology Co., Ltd.** was not the creditor;
- Honghe Boji therefore had no right to assign the claim;
- the alleged claim assignment had not been validly formed;
- Lixin was not an original party to the loan agreement and therefore should not be bound by its jurisdiction clause.

Lixin took the opposite position:

- the original loan agreement provided for jurisdiction where Honghe Boji was located;
- **Lixin described itself as the “claim assignee”;**
- it argued that it had succeeded to all rights and obligations of the original creditor under the loan agreement, including the jurisdiction clause.

At the jurisdiction stage, the court held that the contractual jurisdiction agreement could bind the transferee after assignment and resolved the jurisdiction issue on that basis.

At minimum, the ruling establishes that **by 2018 Lixin was already suing in Haidian in the capacity of a purported claim assignee under loan contracts that included Honghe Boji as a party.**

But the conclusion must remain narrow:

1. the ruling concerns **jurisdiction**, not a merits judgment on the validity of the assignment;
2. the defendants specifically disputed whether Honghe Boji had authority to transfer the claim;
3. the full assignment agreement has not been obtained, so the assignment date, price, original lender, source of funds, and outstanding balance remain unknown;
4. this single case cannot establish that Yooli transferred all or a large share of platform claims to Lixin.

Its importance is that it connects earlier media descriptions of “Yooli acquiring/controlling Lixin” and “Lixin as an asset-side institution” to an **actual litigated chain of claim transfer**.

Source:

- Beijing Haidian District People's Court, (2018) Jing 0108 Min Chu No. 21610, jurisdiction ruling (public republication)  
  https://item.wusiku.top/A/fr/5d/bj/m1.htm

The same group of parties also appears in **(2018) Jing 0108 Min Chu No. 21666** and related cases. Public search results confirm Lixin as plaintiff, but sufficiently complete merits decisions have not yet been obtained to determine whether those cases arose from the same assignment program.

## 2. Puhui Kuaixin's relationship with Yooli can be traced to a formal 2014 strategic cooperation agreement

A Sichuan criminal judgment in an unrelated fraud case quoted historical cooperation documents between Yooli's operating entity and Puhui Kuaixin in detail.

The evidence section states that on **2014-04-10**, Beijing Honghe Boji Financial Information Service Co., Ltd. and Shenzhen Puhui Kuaixin Financial Services Co., Ltd. entered a Strategic Cooperation Agreement.

### Puhui Kuaixin was responsible for

- developing consumer-finance borrowers;
- performing credit review;
- day-to-day management;
- providing borrower information and documents to Yooli under borrower authorization;
- assisting with loan procedures.

### Yooli was responsible for

- developing and managing registered lenders as an internet information-service platform;
- providing information services to borrowers and lenders;
- matching loans;
- receiving borrower repayments and distributing them to lenders.

More importantly, the agreement provided that loans obtained through Yooli could be paid from Yooli's or its branch's settlement account **directly to merchants that cooperated with Puhui Kuaixin and actually supplied the goods or services**.

Subsequent merchant agreements further show that Puhui Kuaixin reviewed and referred consumers to Yooli, after which the consumers applied for loans from platform lenders through Puhui and Yooli.

Thus, by at least 2014, the relationship was not merely an abstract “asset partnership” but a defined chain:

> **offline consumption scenario / merchant → Puhui Kuaixin borrower acquisition and review → Yooli lender funds → merchant receives funds → borrower repays → Yooli distributes repayments to lenders**

Source:

- *He Mou 1, Zheng Mou 1, Yin Mou 1 et al., First-Instance Criminal Judgment for Fraud*, evidence items 84 and 85  
  https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/何某1、郑某1、尹某1等诈骗罪一审刑事判决书

This evidence is unusually useful because it identifies both who produced the consumer-loan assets and how lender money entered the consumption transaction.

## 3. Public statements conflict sharply on how much business Lixin and Puhui Kuaixin supplied

During the late-2015 asset-side staff dispute, different parties disclosed valuable but conflicting figures.

Former Lixin CEO Ye Yanwei said:

- Lixin's 2015 transaction volume was about RMB 3 billion;
- 2014 volume was below RMB 3 billion;
- over two years it had supplied nearly RMB 6 billion of credit assets to Yooli.

Other contemporaneous reports said:

- Lixin supplied more than RMB 3 billion in 2015;
- Puhui Kuaixin supplied more than RMB 2 billion;
- the two together supplied more than RMB 5 billion;
- some market commentary claimed the two institutions represented around 80% of Yooli's asset side.

Wu Yiran publicly rejected that account, stating that the two institutions' actual share of Yooli assets was **below 20%**.

Accordingly, the “80%” or “RMB 5 billion” figures should not be treated as established facts. What can safely be said is that **Lixin and Puhui Kuaixin were among Yooli's largest asset-side providers at the time and that the parties publicly disagreed sharply about their actual share.**

## 4. The most important evidence is now the assignment contract itself, not another corporate chart

The highest-value next step is to pursue the group of cases around **(2018) Jing 0108 Min Chu No. 21610 / 21666** and determine:

1. all parties to the original loan agreement;
2. whether the original funding came from registered Yooli lenders;
3. whether Honghe Boji acted as platform service provider, agent, or a creditor holding transferable rights;
4. whether Lixin acquired the original lender claim, a recovery claim, a service-fee claim, or a package of contractual rights;
5. when the assignment occurred;
6. the consideration paid;
7. whether the same claims later generated enforcement recoveries;
8. if so, which entity ultimately received the recoveries.

A full merits judgment, complaint attachments, assignment contract, or enforcement record could for the first time firmly reconstruct a chain such as “Yooli-platform claim → Lixin asset side → later recovery.”

Until such evidence appears, this project does **not** treat stronger propositions such as “Yooli sold lender claims cheaply to Lixin” as established fact.

## 5. Further review changes the interpretation: later asset-side recovery does not automatically mean “Yooli transferred away its own assets”

A series of Chongqing judgments concerning Yooli and Tongsheng Microcredit explains the early **guarantee compensation → claim assignment** mechanism clearly.

The decisions state that Honghe Boji, as platform operator, developed lenders and matched loans, while Tongsheng developed and reviewed borrowers and provided joint and several guarantees. Their cooperation agreement further provided that:

- after a borrower defaulted, Tongsheng compensated platform lenders;
- **once Tongsheng paid, the lenders' corresponding claims against the borrower automatically and gratuitously transferred to Tongsheng**;
- Tongsheng could sue or further assign those claims;
- Honghe Boji, as the lenders' authorized representative, was required to assist with assignment documentation.

In this structure:

> lender receives guarantee compensation → the lender's claim transfers to the guarantor to the extent of compensation → later amounts recovered by the guarantor from the borrower are, in principle, the guarantor's own recovery assets.

This matters because **the fact that Lixin, Litong, Puhui Kuaixin, or a guarantor later sued a borrower does not by itself mean that those recoveries “should have been paid to Yooli lenders.”** If the corresponding lender had already been fully compensated, the claim was supposed to transfer to the compensating party.

A similar mechanism appears in later Puhui Kuaixin assets. In the Cheng Bin case, Shenzhen Guoyin Shengda Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. paid the relevant loan principal and interest pursuant to a compensation notice issued by Honghe Boji; the court then recognized that the guarantor had acquired the corresponding loan claim and could recover from the borrower.

### A more plausible but still unproven reading of case No. 21610

The guarantee-assignment evidence supports an alternative explanation:

- Honghe Boji may not have been the original creditor;
- it may have been acting under lender authorization to process claim disposal/assignment;
- Lixin may have acquired lender claims rather than “assets owned by Yooli itself.”

Yooli product agreements historically contained broad assignment authorizations. Dingcunbao agreements, for example, authorized Yooli to process claim transfers on lenders' behalf and sign transfer agreements in the lenders' names.

Sources:

- Yooli Dingcunbao lending consultation and service agreement  
  https://www.yooli.com/static/laws/dcbxyStaticV3.jsp
- Yooli claim-transfer agreement  
  https://www.yooli.com/static/laws/zqzr.jsp

The real questions in No. 21610 are therefore:

1. **Who were the original lenders?**
2. **Had they already received compensation or payout before the transfer?**
3. **What legal event triggered Lixin's acquisition?**
4. **Was it a gratuitous post-compensation transfer, or did the platform sell an unpaid lender claim to Lixin?**
5. **If it was a sale, did the lender authorize it, and how much consideration did the lender receive?**

Only abnormalities in categories 4 and 5 would directly connect the transaction to possible wind-down asset loss or lender harm.

This narrows the next investigation substantially: **it is not enough to find evidence that asset-side companies are still collecting old loans. The key is to identify claims belonging to lenders who had not been compensated or paid out, determine to whom those claims were transferred, and establish the transfer price.**
