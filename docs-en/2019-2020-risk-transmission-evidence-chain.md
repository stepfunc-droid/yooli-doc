# Key Evidence Chain for Yooli's Risk Transmission from Late 2019 into 2020

> Updated: 2026-10-16  
> Chinese original: [`2019年末至2020年风险穿透关键证据链.md`](../docs/2019年末至2020年风险穿透关键证据链.md)  
> This article places the evidence that best explains Yooli's rapid transition from “normal exit / zero overdue” in late 2019 to congested claim transfers and large lender-facing overdue amounts in 2020 on a single timeline. The purpose is not to assume a criminal conclusion, but to distinguish and connect three layers: **underlying borrower credit risk, guarantee/compensation buffers, and Dingcunbao claim-transfer liquidity.**
>
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

## 1. 2019-10-31: public metrics still showed “zero overdue,” while cumulative compensation already exceeded RMB 600 million

Yooli's archived operating-information page for **2019-10-31** disclosed:

| Metric | Platform disclosure |
| --- | ---: |
| Outstanding loan balance | **RMB 12.6 billion** |
| Current borrowers | **1,658,632** |
| Outstanding loan count | **2,664,969** |
| Interest balance | **RMB 2,214,950,241.99** |
| Overdue projects | **0** |
| Overdue amount | **RMB 0** |
| Project overdue rate | **0%** |
| Amount over 90 days overdue | **RMB 0** |
| Cumulative compensation | **RMB 602.75 million** |
| Cumulative compensated cases | **86,441** |

Archive:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/statistic/platform/index.html

On that page Yooli defined cumulative compensation as the total amount repaid, because of borrower default or similar reasons, by a third party other than the borrower or the platform.

The figures therefore establish an important mechanism:

> **“Zero overdue on the lender-facing side” did not mean “underlying borrowers never defaulted.” By end-October 2019, Yooli itself had already reported 86,441 compensated cases totaling about RMB 603 million.**

That is consistent with later court decisions involving actual compensation by Guoyin Shengda, Chengxixiang, and other guarantors.

## 2. 2019-11-18: the Dingcunbao page simultaneously said “no transfer-time guarantee” and “historically paid within one business day”

This is one of the most valuable surviving pieces of original product-page evidence.

The `yooli-action` archive restored Yooli's **2019-11-18** Dingcunbao list page and product pages for five service periods. This date matters because these were normal pre-crisis lending pages, not later wind-down pages.

Archives:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/
- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/detail/101.html

### The page clearly showed that the service period was not the maturity of the underlying claim

For the one-month product, the page explained that:

- if the underlying loan had matured when the service period ended, the borrower would repay;
- if an unmatured underlying loan remained, Yooli would initiate a claim transfer under the lender's authorization;
- completion depended on the claim-transfer market;
- **the platform did not promise a transfer completion time**;
- if a transfer could not be completed immediately, the lender might continue holding an underlying claim for as long as **36 months**.

Thus, buying a “one-month Dingcunbao” did not mean the underlying borrower loan itself matured in one month.

### The same page also reinforced an experience close to a fixed-term cash product

The page simultaneously stated:

> **“Historically, funds were received within one business day after the Dingcunbao service period ended.”**

Its meta description characterized Dingcunbao as involving “automatic lending, scheduled automatic transfer, monthly return, and recovery of principal at maturity.”

Both layers of information must be preserved.

The page did disclose transfer-failure and long-holding risk. At the same time, **the long-running user experience and page language reinforced an expectation that the end of the service period would normally be followed quickly by cash receipt.**

It is therefore understandable that many lenders later experienced an inability to exit after the service period as a sudden repayment abnormality.

This is not a legal conclusion about contract enforceability; it explains the gap between formal asset structure and ordinary user risk perception.

## 3. Dingcunbao provided a second layer of risk buffering: guarantee compensation was not the only buffer

Historical agreements show at least two distinct buffers before the crisis.

### Layer one: credit-risk buffer

```text
Underlying borrower becomes delinquent
        ↓
Partner / guarantor compensates, repurchases, or supplies other principal-and-interest protection
        ↓
Lender still receives principal and interest
        ↓
Lender-facing public overdue metric may remain at zero
```

### Layer two: liquidity buffer

```text
Dingcunbao service period ends
        ↓
Underlying loan has not yet matured
        ↓
Platform initiates claim transfer
        ↓
Another user acquires the old claim
        ↓
Original lender receives cash and exits quickly
```

As long as both buffers worked, the ordinary user experience could remain:

> **quick exit after the stated service period + almost no visible overdue.**

That experience alone could not establish that the underlying loan pool remained healthy.

## 4. December 2019: Yooli's public pages still signaled normal operation and business expansion

Yooli's archived homepage from **2019-12-09** still looked like a normal operating platform:

- navigation retained “I want to lend”;
- the page described Yooli as a “six-year professional P2P platform”;
- Dingcunbao and other products remained displayed;
- advertised expected annual returns reached as high as 10%;
- bank depository, small and diversified assets, and full-process risk control were emphasized;
- withdrawals were advertised as potentially arriving in as little as 30 minutes.

Archive:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/

On **2019-12-12**, Yooli's media section also published a report on a strategic cooperation with JD Logistics, framed around expansion into logistics/supply-chain finance scenarios and future multi-level cooperation.

Archive:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/193.html

These pages do not prove that Yooli already knew the eventual scale of the crisis, and they do not establish deliberate concealment.

They do show that **at least in early-to-mid December 2019, the overall picture presented to ordinary users and the public remained one of normal operation, investable products, and business expansion rather than imminent large-scale risk disposal.**

That timing matters when asking when management internally identified a liquidity crisis and when it communicated that change adequately to users.

## 5. Late 2019 to early 2020: claim-transfer liquidity appears to have failed first

In July 2020 media interviews, Yooli customer service confirmed that:

- Dingcunbao users whose service periods had ended were queued for claim transfer according to end date;
- by late July 2020, the platform was still handling some three-month Dingcunbao plans whose service periods had ended on **2020-01-01**;
- Yooli attributed slower transfers in part to fewer lenders and in part to weaker borrower repayment capacity during COVID.

Reports:

- Securities Times: https://news.stcn.com/sd/202007/t20200725_2166328.html
- Nanfang+: https://static.nfnews.com/content/202007/25/c3813683.html

Thus, by July 2020 at least some users had been waiting more than half a year for claim transfer.

Some lenders later said that exit problems became obvious in early December 2019. That remains a lender account rather than a universally established single “failure date,” but it is broadly consistent with Yooli's own acknowledgement that January 2020 service-period expirations remained unprocessed many months later.

## 6. The most important quantitative shift: cumulative compensation rose from RMB 603 million to RMB 4.891 billion

On 2019-10-31 Yooli reported:

> cumulative compensation **RMB 602.75 million**.

By July 2020, platform figures cited publicly had become:

| Metric | July 2020 public data |
| --- | ---: |
| Outstanding loan balance | **RMB 11.418 billion** |
| Current lenders | **160,400** |
| Cumulative compensation | **RMB 4.891 billion** |
| Overdue projects | **269,879** |
| Overdue amount | **RMB 2.678 billion** |
| Project overdue rate | **13.60%** |

From end-October 2019 to July 2020, cumulative compensation increased by approximately:

> **RMB 4.891bn − RMB 0.60275bn = RMB 4.28825bn.**

This is one of the most explanatory public data series in the case.

It means that in less than a year, the historical amount absorbed through third-party compensation increased by about **RMB 4.29 billion**. By June–July 2020, compensation was no longer sufficient to isolate all risk from lenders, and public lender-facing overdue had reached roughly **RMB 2.678 billion**.

A more precise description is therefore not “tens of billions of bad loans suddenly appeared in 2020,” but:

> **before risk appeared directly as lender-facing overdue, the guarantee/compensation system had already absorbed a very large increase in default pressure; later even that buffer could no longer maintain the previous low-overdue appearance.**

Cumulative compensation is a historical cumulative measure. It is not identical to a point-in-time cash loss and does not, by itself, identify funding sources, later recoveries, or any misuse of funds.

## 7. June 2020: the problem developed from “difficulty exiting” into visible deterioration in underlying repayment

Media reports citing National Internet Finance Association / platform data said that at end-June 2020:

- current borrowers: **1,379,331**;
- only **5,556 fewer** than at end-May;
- in preceding months the borrower count had typically declined by roughly **70,000–80,000 per month**;
- overdue amount: **RMB 2.678 billion**;
- increase from end-May: about **RMB 1.05 billion**;
- project overdue rate: **13.60%**;
- amount overdue rate: **20.22%**.

On **2020-06-30**, Yooli stopped new loan disbursements.

At this point the crisis could no longer be explained only as “there were not enough buyers for Dingcunbao claims.”

**A shortage of claim buyers is a liquidity problem; a sudden slowdown in borrower runoff together with a sharp rise in overdue amount indicates deterioration in recovery from the underlying assets themselves.**

## 8. Overlaying asset-side and guarantee-side events makes the 2020 transmission process more complete

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 2019-10-31 | Platform still reported zero overdue, but cumulative compensation had already reached RMB 603m |
| 2019-11-18 | Normal Dingcunbao page showed reliance on claim transfer for exit while also advertising a historical one-business-day cash-return experience |
| Dec. 2019 | Some lenders later reported slower exits; public Yooli pages still signaled normal operation and expansion |
| Feb. 2020 | Guoyin Shengda approved to reduce registered capital from RMB 700m to RMB 300m |
| 2020-04-03 | Puhui Kuaixin announced capital reduction from RMB 100m to RMB 30m |
| 2020-04-24 | Yooli's directly invested Shenzhen Honghe Boji Commercial Factoring entered liquidation |
| 2020-04-24 | Multiple Chengxixiang historical recovery judgments were reopened, vacated, and transferred to police because the company “may be suspected of a crime” |
| May 2020 | 51% of Hemei Zhike judicially auctioned for RMB 2m because of its parent group's debt problems |
| May–Jun. 2020 | Public overdue increased rapidly and borrower runoff slowed materially |
| 2020-06-30 | Yooli stopped new loan disbursements |
| Jul. 2020 | Cumulative compensation about RMB 4.891bn; overdue about RMB 2.678bn; long transfer queues became public |
| Dec. 2020 | 51% of Hemei Microcredit entered judicial auction |
| Mar. 2021 | Formal regulatory risk warning concerning Guoyin Shengda |

These events still cannot be combined into a single proven causal conspiracy or unified cause.

But the time structure is clear:

> **claim-transfer exit liquidity came under pressure first in late 2019; during early-to-mid 2020, underlying credit risk and the asset-partner / guarantee network also showed repeated signs of strain; by May–July the two types of risk were reinforcing each other and passing directly through to lenders.**

## 9. The most complete explanatory model currently supported by the evidence

```text
2018–2019
some underlying borrowers already default
        ↓
guarantee / compensation / repurchase absorbs credit risk
        ↓
lender-facing metrics can still show low or even zero overdue

At the same time

Dingcunbao service period < remaining term of some underlying loans
        ↓
exit after service period depends on another user acquiring the old claim
        ↓
normal years create a stable user experience of receiving cash soon after service-period end

2019 industry-wide balance reduction
        ↓
fewer new lenders and weaker demand for old claims
        ↓
late 2019–early 2020 claim-transfer queues emerge

2020
underlying borrower collections deteriorate further
        +
guarantee / asset-partner network comes under pressure
        ↓
compensation demand surges
        ↓
cumulative compensation rises from about RMB 603m to RMB 4.891bn
        ↓
credit-risk buffer can no longer fully absorb underlying defaults

Meanwhile
old claims become harder to sell to the next lender
        ↓
liquidity buffer also fails

Finally
large numbers of service-period expirations cannot cash out + lender-facing overdue appears
        ↓
discount transfers / whole-account sales / mall / look-through claims / AMC / locality-based recognition
```

This model can simultaneously explain:

1. why Yooli could still report “zero overdue” in 2019;
2. why later serious-default notices contained large amounts of older problem assets;
3. why users first experienced “my service period ended but I cannot exit,” rather than instantly seeing every underlying loan as delinquent;
4. why overdue figures jumped in May–July 2020;
5. why, after risk exposure, Yooli increasingly emphasized that the claims still belonged to lenders and eventually shifted to underlying-claim and AMC disposal mechanisms.

## 10. The most valuable next task is to reconstruct the monthly funding bridge from 2019-12 through 2020-06

The investigation now needs four monthly series:

1. **actual borrower cash repayments;**
2. **actual compensation paid by guarantors / partners;**
3. **new lender inflows and claim-transfer transaction volume;**
4. **new loan disbursements.**

If those four lines can be reconstructed month by month, the central question becomes measurable:

> **Of the cash paid to exiting lenders each month before the crisis fully surfaced, how much came from normal underlying repayment, how much from guarantee compensation, and how much from other lenders acquiring old claims?**

Some lender-created statistics on “new funds, new disbursements, and claim-transfer amounts” exist online, but their original sources and definitions have not yet been independently verified, and some appear to use metrics different from Yooli's public cumulative transaction figures. They are therefore not treated as established facts in this article.

Once these monthly series can be verified, the late-2019-to-mid-2020 transmission process could move from a highly plausible mechanism reconstruction to a quantitatively testable event history.
