# Yooli Consumer-Loan Samples: Nominal Principal vs. Actual Principal

> Updated: 2026-10-16  
> Chinese original: [`有利网消费贷名义本金与实际本金样本.md`](../docs/有利网消费贷名义本金与实际本金样本.md)  
> This article extracts a small set of public court cases in the Yooli–Puhui Kuaixin–Chengxixiang consumer-installment chain where the actual commodity / financing amount, nominal loan principal on Yooli, and one-time loan-management fee can all be observed. The purpose is to test one specific question: **how far did the “loan principal” stated in Yooli contracts differ from the economic value actually received by the borrower?**
>
> **Important limitation:** the current sample contains only seven loans, concentrated in mobile-phone installment products from 2015–2016. It cannot be extrapolated to Yooli’s platform-wide average fee level and cannot be used directly to estimate total bad-debt principal. It does show that the structure was not an isolated case and provides a baseline for expanding the sample.  
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

\* In the Liu case, Chengxixiang used RMB 7,615 as the basis of its compensation claim. The court separately reviewed the management fee and part of the interest and did not simply support the full amount claimed.

Across these seven samples:

- the one-time management fee was **20%–40%** of the commodity / actual amount;
- the median was about **30%**;
- the arithmetic mean was about **29.3%**;
- stated differently, nominal principal in these contracts exceeded the commodity value actually received by the borrower by about **20%–40%**.

If one directly compares Chengxixiang’s final claimed / paid compensation with commodity value, the ratio in this small sample is roughly **1.8–2.3 times**. That figure also contains contractual interest and overdue interest, so it should not be simplified into “the asset side extracted twice the amount in fees.”

## 2. Why these figures matter

### 2.1 Yooli’s disclosed “loan principal” was not necessarily the borrower’s real economic principal

Take the Zhang case:

- actual mobile-phone financing amount: RMB 4,888;
- principal stated in the Yooli loan agreement: RMB 6,110;
- of that, RMB 1,222 was included up front as a loan-management fee collected by Puhui Kuaixin and a third-party service provider.

From the lender side, the claim appeared as **RMB 6,110 of principal**, even though the borrower received a commodity worth only **RMB 4,888**.

If this structure was widespread, Yooli’s cumulative lending, outstanding principal, and overdue principal would be different metrics from the actual cash or commodity value borrowers received.

### 2.2 Courts did not automatically treat all of these fees as recoverable principal

Courts in different jurisdictions scrutinized this structure.

In the Liu case, for example, the court held that the RMB 1,155 management fee exceeded the amount the borrower had applied to borrow and that the plaintiff failed to prove that the fee standard and amount had been adequately disclosed and agreed. The court therefore refused to treat the fee and corresponding interest as fully recoverable.

An empirical study by Peking University’s Financial Law Research Center covering 4,531 online-lending intermediation-fee judgments also cited a Yooli–Puhui Kuaixin–Chengxixiang case involving a RMB 3,690 commodity price and RMB 738 management fee. The fee was 20% of the actual financing amount, and the court treated it as part of the borrowing cost requiring judicial review together with interest.

This means:

> **Contractual nominal claim balance was not necessarily equal to the principal that could ultimately be recovered in full through judicial enforcement.**

## 3. Possible implications for Yooli’s later asset quality

The sample points to at least three questions worth testing further.

### 3.1 “Bad-debt principal” may itself include a meaningful amount of front-loaded fees

If a real consumer purchase of about RMB 4,000 was transformed into a platform principal balance above RMB 5,000, then after default the platform’s nominal bad-debt principal would already be larger than the actual commodity / cash value the borrower had received.

That creates a potential hierarchy:

> **contract principal > actual value received by borrower > principal ultimately supported in court**

### 3.2 The asset side may have realized part of its economics at origination

The cases expressly state that management fees were collected by **Puhui Kuaixin and third-party service providers** and were incorporated into nominal principal at origination.

That raises further questions:

- how those fees were distributed among Puhui Kuaixin, Yooli, guarantee companies, and other service providers;
- whether asset-side entities obtained much of their service income at loan origination;
- which entity ultimately bore later bad debt, guarantee-compensation cost, and wind-down losses.

The public judgments generally say only that the fee was collected by “Puhui Kuaixin and third-party service providers” and do not disclose the complete revenue split.

### 3.3 This may help explain why some wind-down assets had extremely low recovery value

If nominal principal already contained front-loaded fees of 20%–40%, and the same asset later faced:

- weak borrower repayment capacity;
- prolonged delinquency;
- incomplete judicial support for high service / management fees;
- collection and enforcement costs;

then a low discounted disposal price for nominal claims would not be surprising.

This does not prove asset transfer. It provides a concrete asset-side explanation for why some remaining nominal claims could have very poor economic value.

## 5. Next step

These seven loans are enough to show that “management fees included in platform nominal principal” was a recurring product structure, but not enough to represent the whole platform.

The most useful expansion would focus on:

- 2015–2017 Puhui Kuaixin consumer installments;
- Lixin Kuaijie cash / small unsecured loans;
- Litong auto loans;
- different asset-side channels, years, and amounts ultimately supported by courts.

The objective is not sample size for its own sake. It is to answer two questions directly relevant to the wind-down:

1. **How much of Yooli’s historical “principal balance” may actually have consisted of front-loaded service, management, or similar fees rather than real cash / commodity value delivered to borrowers?**
2. **Which asset-side or service entities ultimately received those fees?**
