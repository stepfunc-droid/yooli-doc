# Yooli Event Timeline

> Compiled: 2026-10-13  
> Chinese original: [`有利网事件时间线.md`](../docs/有利网事件时间线.md)  
> This timeline reconstructs Yooli's transition from normal operation and industry-wide balance reduction to claim-transfer congestion, cessation of new origination, formal P2P exit, and later claim disposal.  
> To avoid turning inference into fact, each stage distinguishes among **official/original materials**, **media reports citing platform or regulatory data**, **lender complaints/media retellings**, and **analytical inference**.
>
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

---

## 1. 2012–2015: establishment and rapid expansion

### 2012-05-31 | Operating entity established

Beijing Honghe Boji Financial Information Service Co., Ltd., the principal Yooli operating entity, was established.

**Evidence type: public corporate-registration information / media reporting.**

### 2013-02-25 | Yooli launched

Yooli formally began operations.

The platform subsequently completed multiple financing rounds and expanded rapidly, becoming one of the larger P2P platforms of its era.

### 2013–2015 | Multiple venture-capital rounds

Public reports show investments by SoftBank China, Morningside, Hillhouse, and other institutions. By 2016, cumulative matched lending had already reached the tens-of-billions-of-renminbi range.

The significance of this period is that Yooli was not a short-lived small platform. By the industry's peak, it had accumulated a large stock of lenders, borrowers, and loan assets.

### January 2014 | Chairman interview and long-running “100% principal-and-interest protection / guarantee” messaging

In January 2014, Sina Tech republished an interview by *China Business Times* with Yooli chairman Ren Yong. The report described Yooli products as carrying “100% principal-and-interest guarantee” and explained that offline microloan and other partner institutions performed credit checks and underwriting and assumed joint guarantee liability.

During the same period and later, Yooli's website and Dingcunbao pages repeatedly used expressions such as “100% principal-and-interest protection” and “100% free guarantee by major partner institutions.”

This is important because early lender risk perception was not formed solely around the abstract idea that “P2P lenders bear their own risk.” It was also shaped over time by strong platform messaging about principal-and-interest protection and partner guarantees.

**Evidence type: archived Yooli pages / media interview.**

- Sina Tech: http://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2014-01-14/10369095260.shtml

### 2015–2016 | Public shift toward the asset side and a “credit factory” acquisition/integration strategy

A 2015 *Yicai* report described Yooli management as planning to move “from a pure platform toward the asset side.” By 2016, media reports said Yooli had completed or was pursuing acquisition/integration of Lixin Kuaijie, Puhui Kuaixin, and other asset-originating institutions, with the strategy described as building a consumer-finance “credit factory.”

Later court decisions further confirm that Puhui Kuaixin, Litong Auto Loans, and other institutions actually performed borrower acquisition, review, referral, post-loan management, collateral/GPS work, and collection.

This does not by itself prove self-financing. It does show that later Yooli was more than a website entirely separated from loan production.

**Evidence type: contemporaneous financial-media reporting + later judicial decisions.**

## 2. 2017–2018: compliance, pilot programs, and the formation of a “leading compliant platform” image

### June 2017 | First-batch participant in National Internet Finance Association information-disclosure system

The National Internet Finance Association launched its Internet Finance Registration and Disclosure Service Platform, with Yooli among the first ten pilot institutions.

A necessary distinction: this was an **industry self-regulatory information-disclosure pilot**. It did not mean that the local financial regulator had completed Yooli's formal P2P filing, and it was not the later “regulatory pilot.”

For ordinary lenders, however, “first-batch pilot institution” naturally carried a strong compliance-signaling effect.

Contemporaneous media summaries of the first-batch platform data showed Yooli's project and amount overdue rates as **0**.

### December 2017 | First-batch connection to product registration

When the National Internet Finance Association's product-registration function launched, Yooli again appeared among the first connected platforms.

This reinforced its image as an institution near the front of the industry's normalization and disclosure infrastructure, but again should not be rewritten as “Yooli completed P2P filing.”

### Mid-2018 | Industry risk clearing begins; Yooli's stock starts a sustained decline

Phoenix WEMONEY later reported that from **June 2018 to May 2019**, Yooli's outstanding balance fell **34.66%**.

Yooli's April 2021 retrospective announcement said that at the start of “risk clearing and stock resolution”:

- principal receivable was nearly **RMB 21 billion**;
- existing lenders numbered more than **260,000**.

Viewed together with later balance declines, the high point of risk reduction was **probably around mid-2018**. This is an analytical inference from several public data series; Yooli's 2021 announcement did not identify an exact date.

**Stage assessment: from roughly mid-2018 onward, Yooli had moved from expansion into sustained stock reduction.**

### End-2018 | CEO continued emphasizing compliant leading-platform status and contractual repayment

In December 2018, founder and CEO Wu Yiran said in an industry review that:

- 2018 was a process of industry “sorting and clearing”;
- Yooli had “consistently adhered to compliant development”;
- lenders still received contractual repayments during the industry shock “without any discount”;
- after the industry test, compliant leading platforms would have better development prospects in 2019.

Against a backdrop in which many platforms were expected to disappear, such statements could reasonably support a belief that **risk primarily concerned noncompliant institutions and that Yooli might be among the leading platforms that survived.**

Source:

https://www.cebnet.com.cn/20181228/102543035.html

### End-2018 | Outstanding loan balance about RMB 15.4 billion

Public media citing platform data reported an end-2018 outstanding loan balance of approximately **RMB 15.4 billion**.

Compared with the later-retrospective peak near RMB 21 billion, Yooli had already reduced a substantial amount of stock by this point.

## 3. 2019: stock reduction continued while filing, compliance, and safety expectations were repeatedly reinforced

### 2019-01-24 | Yooli “risk education” page publishes “broad development space after filing”

Yooli's risk-education section published *Chinese Academy of Social Sciences: P2P Will Still Have Broad Development Space After Filing Is Implemented*. The article argued that compliant P2P platforms could continue to develop after regulatory filing.

This was a republished educational article, not a Yooli promise that it would itself obtain filing. But the platform's choice to display the article formed part of the information environment presented to users.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/121.html

### April 2019 | Outstanding balance RMB 14.37 billion; current lenders about 209,510

Public information including 01Caijing showed:

- outstanding loan balance about **RMB 14.37 billion**;
- current lenders **209,510**;
- cumulative lending about **RMB 66.832 billion**;
- current borrowers about **1.7526 million**.

Yooli remained in a state of simultaneous normal operation and continuing stock reduction.

### 2019-04-08 | 2018 special audit issued; within its scope, no fund pool or self-financing identified

Beijing Surong Certified Public Accountants issued a special audit of key areas for 2018. Within the audit and sampling procedures described, it stated that it had not identified Yooli as operating a fund pool, advancing money for clients, financing itself or disguisedly financing itself, or selling wealth-management products to raise funds.

The 2018 financial audit meanwhile showed approximately:

- cash: **RMB 128 million**;
- other receivables: **RMB 206 million**;
- prepayments: **RMB 28 million**;
- total liabilities: **RMB 402 million**;
- equity: about **negative RMB 31 million**.

Both types of evidence should be preserved. The special audit did not identify prohibited fund-pool or self-financing conduct within its procedures, while the nature and counterparties of large receivable/payable balances still require look-through analysis. The financial-statement figures alone do not establish fund diversion.

**Evidence type: original audit reports.**

### 2019-04-15 | Website publishes a “filing countdown” article

Yooli's risk-education section published *Filing Countdown: P2P to Reopen in June; Beijing Administrative Verification to End*, discussing the market-circulated conditional filing pilot proposal, Beijing administrative verification, and expectations for filing pilots in the second half of the year.

The article itself indicated that the filing-pilot proposal remained a market-circulated document and did not show that regulators had formally approved Yooli's filing.

But the communication effect remains relevant: during a highly uncertain period, Yooli's website continued presenting content suggesting that filing was approaching and compliant platforms might survive.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/education/detail/128.html

### 2019-06-30 | Outstanding balance RMB 13.986 billion; current lenders 205,900

Phoenix WEMONEY cited public Yooli data showing:

- cumulative lending **RMB 67.513 billion**;
- outstanding loan balance **RMB 13.986 billion**;
- current lenders **205,900**;
- current borrowers **1.7575 million**.

Compared with the mid-2018 high point, lender count and balance had fallen materially, but the platform was still operating.

### June–July 2019 | Ownership and registered-capital restructuring

Public corporate and court materials show that Yooli's operating entity moved from direct natural-person ownership by Wu Yiran and Ren Yong to 100% ownership by **Beijing Honghe Boji Technology Co., Ltd.** in late June 2019.

Two days after the 2019-06-26 transfer documents, the operating entity's registered capital increased from **RMB 500 million to RMB 1 billion**. Contemporaneous media reporting said paid-in capital remained **RMB 50 million**.

Later litigation shows that Ren Yong denied signing or authorizing some key 2018 capital-increase and 2019 equity-transfer documents. The courts whose public decisions are currently available did **not** decide the authenticity of those signatures on the merits.

See:

- [Shareholder Relationship Between Wu Yiran and Ren Yong, and the 2018–2019 Corporate-Governance Disputes](./wuyiran-renyong-governance-disputes-2018-2019.md)

### July 2019 | Regulators introduce the “mature one, include one” principle for regulatory pilots

A national online-lending risk-rectification meeting said that after compliance inspection, system connection, and data verification, institutions basically qualified after rectification could be included in regulatory pilots under the principle **“mature one, include one.”**

This was an official policy direction, but the public document **did not name Yooli as an approved pilot institution**.

Official source:

https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-07/07/c_1124719562.htm

The same policy period also emphasized the industry's “three reductions,” transformation, and orderly exit, so the regulatory environment was mixed: a small possible survival path for qualified institutions coexisted with broad stock reduction and exit.

### 2019-09-04 | Yooli publicly characterizes the regulatory-pilot policy as favorable to itself

Yooli's archived article *Accumulating Small Amounts into Scale: Yooli's Small-and-Diversified Model Surpasses RMB 100 Billion in Transactions* is a key source.

Alongside the RMB 100 billion transaction milestone, it emphasized:

- “steady operation”;
- small, diversified and real-scenario assets;
- “adherence to compliant development”;
- “comprehensive information disclosure”;
- not touching the 13 regulatory red lines since launch;
- full Huaxia Bank fund depository;
- registered capital increased to RMB 1 billion, described as improving risk resistance;
- first-batch participation in the 2017 National Internet Finance Association information-disclosure system;
- first-batch connection in May 2019 to the National Computer Network Emergency Response Technical Team / Coordination Center's real-time data reporting system.

Most notably, after discussing “mature one, include one,” the article stated:

> “This is undoubtedly a major positive for online-lending platforms such as Yooli that have consistently adhered to compliant development.”

It concluded that Yooli would continue compliant development and transparent operation and provide “safe, authentic and trustworthy” products and services.

This was not a direct claim that Yooli had already received regulatory-pilot approval, but it clearly **linked the future regulatory-pilot prospect to Yooli's own compliance identity.**

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/media/detail/191.html

### 2019-10-31 | Balance about RMB 12.6 billion; cumulative compensation about RMB 603 million; overdue still displayed as zero

Yooli's archived operating page disclosed:

- outstanding loan balance about **RMB 12.6 billion**;
- current borrowers **1,658,632**;
- outstanding loan count **2,664,969**;
- interest balance about **RMB 2.215 billion**;
- cumulative compensation **RMB 602.75 million**;
- cumulative compensated cases **86,441**;
- overdue projects, overdue amount, and project overdue rate all **0**.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/statistic/platform/index.html

“Zero overdue” should not automatically be labeled false. Under regulatory disclosure definitions, if a third party compensates after borrower default so that the lender receives principal and interest on time, the lender-facing overdue field may remain at zero.

The deeper question is that **by October 2019 at least roughly RMB 600 million had already required third-party compensation, proving underlying borrower default was not zero, while the most intuitive user-facing overdue metrics remained zero and the platform was still emphasizing compliance, safety, regulatory connectivity, and risk resistance. Whether underlying default and compensation pressure were communicated in a sufficiently complete, timely, and understandable way is a separate disclosure question.**

Official disclosure rule:

https://www.jsjrb.gov.cn/art/2017/8/30/art_34_25406.html

### 2019-11-18 | Dingcunbao page says both “no transfer-time guarantee” and “historically within one business day”

The restored normal-operation Dingcunbao pages state that when the service period ends and the underlying loan remains unmatured, a claim transfer must be initiated; completion depends on assignee demand and **the platform does not guarantee transfer time**.

Yet the same one-month plan page also states: **“Historically, funds were received within one business day after the Dingcunbao service period ended.”** Product descriptions also used phrases such as “scheduled automatic transfer” and “principal recovered at maturity.”

The page therefore preserves both the true contractual structure and ordinary user perception: transfer-failure risk was disclosed, but historical experience and marketing language reinforced an expectation of quick exit after the service period.

Archives:

- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/
- https://wayback.yooli-action.online/dingcunbao/detail/101.html

### 2019-12-09 | Website still presented a complete normal-operation picture

An archived homepage still showed “I want to lend,” Dingcunbao products, expected annual returns up to about 10%, bank depository, risk-control messaging, and a claim that withdrawals could arrive in as little as 30 minutes.

This was very close to the period in which lenders later reported materially slower claim transfers, yet the overall public-facing state still looked like normal operation with investable products.

### 2019-12-12 | Website continues publishing JD Logistics strategic-cooperation report

Yooli's media section published a report on strategic cooperation with JD Logistics, still framed around expanding logistics and supply-chain-finance scenarios and conducting further multi-level cooperation.

This does not establish what management internally knew about the eventual crisis size, but it shows that significant business-expansion signals remained public in December 2019.

## 4. Late 2019 through mid-2020: claim-transfer liquidity crisis becomes visible

### Around December 2019 | Many lenders later report materially slower maturity exits

In July 2020 media interviews, multiple Yooli lenders said that **from around December 2019, funds at the end of the product service period could no longer exit as previously expected**.

This date comes primarily from lender complaints and media interviews; it is not a platform-announced default date.

Later Yooli customer-service explanations are consistent with the mechanism: Dingcunbao exit required **claim transfer**, not direct platform cash payment, and when assignment capacity declined users entered queues according to service-period end date.

Accordingly, **around December 2019 is a useful practical boundary between “stock reduction with relatively functional exits” and “serious claim-transfer congestion,” while remaining an approximate rather than legally fixed date.**

### 2020-01-14 | Chengxixiang receives renewed financing-guarantee license

A Shenzhen local financial-regulator announcement shows Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. met renewal requirements and received a renewed financing-guarantee business license.

This means that at least administratively in January 2020, an important guarantor in Yooli's consumer-loan chain was still treated as a licensed institution permitted to continue operations.

### 2020-02-21 | Guoyin Shengda capital reduction approved: RMB 700 million → RMB 300 million

Shenzhen regulators approved Shenzhen Guoyin Shengda Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd.'s registered-capital reduction from **RMB 700 million to RMB 300 million**.

Multiple court decisions confirm Guoyin Shengda provided guarantees for some Puhui Kuaixin consumer loans and Litong auto loans referred to Yooli, later obtaining recovery rights after compensation.

No evidence currently proves that the capital reduction was intended to avoid Yooli-related compensation liabilities. It remains an important risk-timeline event at a key guarantee node.

### 2020-04-03 | Puhui Kuaixin announces capital reduction: RMB 100 million → RMB 30 million

*New Express Daily* published a capital-reduction notice for Shenzhen Puhui Kuaixin Financial Services Co., Ltd., reducing registered capital from **RMB 100 million to RMB 30 million**.

Because Puhui Kuaixin is repeatedly confirmed in court decisions as a core Yooli consumer-finance asset-side institution, the change is more relevant than capital reductions by peripheral companies. Temporal proximity alone, however, does not establish asset transfer.

### 2020-04-24 | Shenzhen Honghe Boji Commercial Factoring enters liquidation; Chengxixiang recovery cases transferred to police the same day

A *New Express Daily* notice shows Shenzhen Honghe Boji Commercial Factoring Co., Ltd., directly invested in by Yooli's operating entity, entered liquidation.

On the same date, Huai'an Intermediate Court handled several historical Chengxixiang recovery cases. Prior civil judgments were vacated, Chengxixiang's suits dismissed, and matters transferred to police because the court said **“Chengxixiang may be suspected of a crime.”**

The events came from separate legal chains, and **there is currently no evidence that they were connected or part of a common arrangement**. But the coincidence of liquidation at a directly invested factoring company and judicial abnormality at a core guarantor immediately before Yooli's public crisis is worth preserving on the timeline.

### May 2020 | 51% of Hemei Zhike judicially auctioned and transferred

Hemei Group disclosed that its 51% stake in Shenzhen Hemei Zhihui Technology Co., Ltd. was judicially auctioned because of the parent group's debt dispute. The company had net assets of about **negative RMB 397 million** at disposal.

Hemei Zhike and Hemei Microcredit had been important early Yooli external asset/guarantee partners. Listed-company statements still showed around **RMB 92.32 million** of Yooli-channel outstanding loans at 2020-06-30.

This does not prove Hemei's crisis caused Yooli's risk. It does show that part of Yooli's historical asset/guarantee network was itself under operating and judicial stress at the same time.

### 2020-06-25 | First serious-default borrower list published

Yooli published its first batch of serious-default borrowers. Media reported that the platform began publicly pursuing overdue borrowers and said relevant delinquency data would be reported to regulators and credit-reporting systems.

This marks a shift from the earlier lender-facing “zero overdue” presentation to large-scale public handling of underlying delinquency.

### 2020-06-30 | New loan origination stopped

Yooli told media in July 2020 that:

> the platform had **stopped new loan disbursements on 2020-06-30**.

This is a clear operating-stage transition. Thereafter the platform was effectively resolving existing stock rather than expanding through new matching activity.

### 2020-07-24 to 07-27 | Transfer congestion becomes public; some January expirations still unprocessed

Yooli customer service told media that:

- Dingcunbao exit depended on claim transfer;
- users were queued by service-period end date;
- in late July 2020 the platform was still handling some plans whose service periods had ended on **2020-01-01**;
- for the same end date, plans with longer original terms could receive priority.

The exit queue was therefore at least about half a year behind for some users, indicating that claim-transfer liquidity had lost its previous normal function.

Public figures around that time were approximately:

| Metric | Late July 2020 |
| --- | ---: |
| Outstanding loan balance | **RMB 11.418 billion** |
| Current lenders | **about 160,400** |
| Interest balance | **more than RMB 1.8 billion** |
| Overdue amount | **RMB 2.678 billion** |
| Project overdue rate | **13.60%** |
| Amount overdue rate | **20.22%** |
| Cumulative compensation | **about RMB 4.891 billion** |

Compared with the approximately RMB 603 million cumulative compensation shown in October 2019, the figure had risen dramatically.

### 2020-07-31 | Website “Compliance Operation / Security” page still retained strong reassurance

The archived Yooli security page contains data through **2020-07-31** while still displaying phrases such as:

- “Yooli has operated steadily for more than 2,000 days”;
- “small and diversified, effectively reducing risk”;
- “careful selection, only high-quality borrowers”;
- “layered safeguards, ensuring repayment on time”;
- bank depository, capital backing, and risk-control-system messaging.

Archive:

https://wayback.yooli-action.online/static/security/index.html

This may have been a static page retained from earlier periods, so its continued availability does not prove that Yooli newly issued those statements in July 2020.

It does establish that **after transfer congestion had become severe and overdue had reached billions of renminbi, a core website “security” page still contained language that could materially reduce users' perception of risk.**

### July 2020 | Public controversy intensifies

Because entertainer Yang Di had previously appeared in Yooli promotional material, many lenders used his social-media pages to publicize difficulties exiting Yooli, bringing the event into wider public view.

Securities Times, China Fund News, and other outlets reported:

- claim-transfer queues;
- inability to exit promptly after service-period expiry;
- rapidly increasing overdue amounts;
- discounted exit schemes;
- lender questions about fund flows, compensation, and continuing loan activity.

Claims of “retained repayments,” “fund pool,” or “asset transfer” during this period came largely from lender complaints. Without bank records, a judicial investigation, or forensic audit, they should not be presented as established fact.

### Second half of 2020 | Exit routes become increasingly wind-down oriented

Yooli increasingly used whole-account discounted transfers and special exit channels. Exit no longer meant simply waiting for Dingcunbao to end and transfer normally; discounts, collection, and asset disposal became more important.

Later media also cited public information from Haidian Subdistrict indicating that local authorities had conducted onsite inspections and interviews and required the company to implement financial-risk resolution responsibilities.

**Stage assessment: by the second half of 2020, Yooli had effectively entered wind-down mode.**

### 2020-11-23 | Nearly five months after new origination stopped, Dingcunbao still accepted new lender funds

A *21st Century Business Herald* reporter found that Yooli still displayed **3-, 6-, 12-, and 24-month** Dingcunbao products accepting funds, with expected annual returns around **6%–10%**.

This did not necessarily contradict the June 30 cessation of new loan origination because historical Dingcunbao agreements allowed new money to **acquire existing claims held by other lenders**. Current public evidence does not prove these funds created new borrower principal.

The important look-through question is which claims the new funds acquired, when those loans were originally disbursed, whether they already had delinquency/compensation/extension records, and who the selling lenders were.

### December 2020 | 51% of Hemei Microcredit enters judicial auction

Another core financial subsidiary in the Hemei system, Hemei Microcredit, entered a judicial auction process for its 51% controlling stake. Later enforcement procedures created disputes over completion/cancellation, so the event should be described as an auction process rather than an undisputed final transfer.

## 5. 2021: formal exit from P2P matching

### 2021-02-26 | Chengxixiang's 2019 annual regulatory review still shown as “passed”

Shenzhen regulators published 2019 annual-review results for financing-guarantee institutions, with Chengxixiang marked **“annual review passed.”**

This does not negate the Huai'an court transfers. Administrative licensing/annual review and a court's suspicion concerning particular transactions are distinct processes.

### March 2021 | Formal regulatory risk warning concerning Guoyin Shengda

Shenzhen regulators stated that Guoyin Shengda's financing-guarantee license had been valid through **2020-09-17**, and that actual controller Li Baoxiang had been listed as a **dishonest judgment debtor and subject to consumption restrictions**.

This was a formal risk signal at another important historical Yooli guarantor.

### 2021-04-01 | *Recent Update on the Company*

This is one of the most important wind-down announcements located.

Yooli stated that as of **end-March 2021**:

- cumulative lending facilitated: **RMB 74.5 billion**;
- borrowers served: about **5 million**;
- principal receivable at the start of risk clearing / stock resolution: nearly **RMB 21 billion**;
- existing lenders at that starting point: more than **260,000**;
- lenders with “full recovery of principal and interest”: **150,000**;
- remaining outstanding loan balance: about **RMB 8 billion**;
- remaining lenders: **fewer than 110,000**;
- compensation already generated: about **RMB 5 billion**;
- platform-described bad debt: nearly **RMB 7 billion**;
- platform-described underlying overdue rate: nearly **90%**.

Yooli formally announced exit from online-lending information-intermediary matching and proposed four stock-resolution routes:

1. pass-through of underlying recoveries;
2. whole-account transfer;
3. shopping mall;
4. packages combining some cash and some mall value.

This marks the transition from de facto wind-down to **public formal exit from P2P matching**.

The contrast with 2019 is sharp: in 2019 Yooli was still emphasizing regulatory-pilot prospects, compliant-leading-platform status, transparent operation, and “safe, authentic and trustworthy” service; by 2021 it was acknowledging underlying overdue near 90% and bad debt near RMB 7 billion.

This does not prove that management already knew the entire 2021 loss magnitude in 2019. It strengthens the question of **whether there was a significant time gap between the speed of internal risk deterioration and the risk information communicated to lenders.**

### 2021-07-22 | Plan to establish a lender representative assembly

An archived Yooli notice stated:

> “Since Yooli entered wind-down mode last year pursuant to policy requirements...”

It also described problems during the wind-down including:

- major staff losses;
- weak collection;
- debt evasion;
- legacy bad debts;
- stalled litigation.

The platform proposed a Yooli lender representative assembly to create a later communication mechanism.

This notice further confirms that **Yooli itself viewed 2020 as the beginning of wind-down mode.**

### July–September 2021 | Wind-down services continued

Archived 2021 website notices show continuing customer-service, holiday, and service-adjustment announcements.

A 2021-09-30 notice shows services still including:

- whole-account rights transfer based on net deposit/withdrawal difference;
- whole-account rights transfer based on principal receivable;
- whole-account shopping-mall exchange;
- withdrawals.

By this stage Yooli functioned less as a normal P2P matching platform and more as a **stock-disposal platform centered on rights transfers, mall conversion, and wind-down processing.**

## 6. Late 2021–2022: cash exit gives way further to claim recognition

### 2021-12-28 | “Final debt payout plan” introduces locality-based claim recognition

Media reported that Yooli published what it called a “final debt payout plan” centered on **locality-based debt-claim recognition**.

In broad terms:

- lenders could recognize underlying claims in selected localities at a nominal 1:1 ratio to account principal receivable;
- after recognition they could wait for borrower payment, collect directly, or later sell at a discount;
- some prior cash and mall routes would gradually be withdrawn.

The 1:1 ratio was a **nominal claim amount substitution**, not 1:1 cash repayment by the platform.

The notice also linked changes in the original cash and mall routes to **funding problems at a third-party asset-management company**. In the locality-based structure, the third-party company was more than a cash buyer: it also acted as assignee of original claims, intermediary in the claim pool, and transferor of replacement claims.

Its identity, shareholders, total purchase scale, discounts, and actual recoveries remain undisclosed and are among the most important late-stage counterparties requiring look-through investigation.

### 2022-01-11 | Yooli confirms the cash-channel third-party AMC had a formal cooperation agreement

A Yooli notice republished by media said the third-party asset-management company operating the cash-transfer route had a formal agreement with Yooli and had actually deployed funds to purchase claims. Yooli said purchased claims had **“quality below expectations and poor recoveries,”** so neither party would renew the expiring agreement.

This confirms that the third-party company was not a purely virtual matching role but a real contractual counterparty and claim assignee during the wind-down.

The notice still did not disclose its full legal name, shareholders, average purchase price, total face amount acquired, or actual later recoveries.

### 2022-02-01 | Cash-transfer exit channel closes

Media republications of the January notice state that from **2022-02-01** Yooli closed the cash-transfer exit route and shifted the work focus further toward locality-based claim recognition.

The wind-down model therefore changed again:

> from “platform-organized cash / discounted exit” toward “underlying claims returned to lenders for long-term holding, collection, or self-disposal.”

### 2022 | Shareholder-governance litigation becomes publicly relevant

Public court decisions from 2019–2022 show Ren Yong disputing shareholder registration, 2018 capital-increase documents, 2019 equity-transfer documents, and related corporate resolutions within the Honghe Boji group.

Ren alleged that some signatures were not his and were unauthorized. The available rulings largely resolved jurisdiction, withdrawal, or standing issues and **did not determine signature authenticity on the merits**.

This evidence confirms a significant governance dispute between the two core founder/shareholder camps, but does not prove forgery or criminal conduct.

## 7. 2021–2024: serious-default notices continue, progressively reaching older loan vintages

After the first serious-default borrower notice in June 2020, Yooli continued publishing such notices through **batch 295 on 2024-10-29**.

Structured aggregation of batches 1–295 produces **621,387 official notice records** with cumulative readable “overdue amounts” of about **RMB 9.794 billion by notice appearance**. This is not a point-in-time overdue balance and cannot directly be treated as unique claim principal.

The sequence progressively reaches older originations.

### 2022-01-04 | Batch 102 contains many 2017-originated loans

By batch 102, large numbers of **2017 loans** appeared, showing that the wind-down and collection process had moved into much older assets.

### 2023-02-13 | Batch 214 mainly reaches late-2016 loans

By batch 214, the list mainly contained loans formed in **late 2016**.

### 2024-10-29 | Batch 295 still includes mid-2016 loans

Batch 295 continued to list borrowers more than six months overdue, including examples originated in **mid-2016**. Yooli was therefore still handling problem assets formed many years earlier as of late 2024.

Across all 295 batches:

- loans originated in **2016–2018** account for about **RMB 8.542 billion, 87.2%** of cumulative notice-appearance amount;
- **2017 loans account for about RMB 4.853 billion**, the single largest year;
- by publication year, the **2021 and 2022 notices together account for about RMB 8.772 billion, roughly 89.6%**.

A further final-maturity analysis using loan start date + original term finds that batch 214 lacks term data for 2,000 records; among the remaining 619,387 records, **481,434 records totaling about RMB 6.26570 billion by later notice appearance** had final contractual maturity by **2019-10-31**. **301,479 records totaling about RMB 2.57891 billion** had matured by end-2018. After conservatively removing exact cross-batch strict-fingerprint duplicates, the pre-2019-10-31 maturity set still totals **441,695 records and about RMB 6.11316 billion**.

This maturity analysis is stronger than origination-year analysis alone. A significant share of loans later appearing in serious-default notices had already passed their original contractual final maturity before the 2019 “zero overdue” date. It still does **not** prove they had all defaulted then or had already been classified by Yooli as bad debt; extensions, restructurings, and compensation may have intervened.

Likewise, Yooli's 2021 statement of “nearly RMB 7 billion bad debt” and the later RMB 9.794 billion cumulative notice-appearance total cannot be directly subtracted. Exact strict-fingerprint cross-batch additional appearances account for about **RMB 195.22 million (1.99%)**, but there is still no true unique claim ID and the definitions and dates differ.

## 9. Five stages provide the clearest overall picture

### Stage 1: mid-2018 to mid-2019

**Continuous stock reduction, but exit mechanisms remain broadly functional, while Yooli continues strengthening expectations that compliant leading platforms may survive.**

Outstanding balance and lender counts decline and many earlier-maturing accounts exit normally.

### Stage 2: late 2019 through first half 2020

**Claim-transfer liquidity weakens while underlying default and compensation pressure worsen rapidly.**

This is the period in which many lenders who ultimately did not fully recover become effectively trapped.

### Stage 3: after 2020-06-30

**No new loan origination; focus shifts to existing stock.**

Transfer queues, collection, and risk resolution become the main tasks.

### Stage 4: 2021

**Formal exit from P2P matching and multiple monetization routes.**

Yooli moves from ordinary underlying repayments toward whole-account transfers, shopping-mall exchange, packages, and pass-through collections.

### Stage 5: late 2021 through 2024 and beyond

**Cash-exit capacity weakens further; claim recognition becomes central while historical problem assets continue to be processed and published.**

Underlying claims and long-term collection risk are increasingly returned to lenders, while serious-default notices continue through 2024 and reach loans originated in 2016–2018.

## 10. What this timeline means for the “150,000 fully repaid” figure and the question of why the final 110,000 did not exit

The timeline makes Yooli's “150,000 fully repaid” statement easier to interpret:

- the platform retrospectively described a starting point of **more than 260,000 existing lenders**;
- by March 2021 **fewer than 110,000** remained;
- the difference is numerically around **150,000**;
- this strongly supports the interpretation that the 150,000 were mainly stock lenders who exited during the risk-reduction period, although public materials do not prove that the three figures use perfectly identical population definitions;
- the 2018–2021 path of declining balances and lender counts suggests the exits were spread across the whole reduction period, with a substantial part likely completed before the late-2019 claim-transfer crisis.

But the question “why didn't the final 110,000 run?” cannot be reduced to lender risk preference.

At least two different questions must be separated:

> **First, after late 2019, were some users objectively unable to exit because of product terms and transfer queues? Second, while users still had the opportunity to stop renewing, reduce exposure, or exit, did Yooli's public information allow an ordinary lender to understand how rapidly the underlying risk was deteriorating?**

Current evidence establishes that during 2018–2019 Yooli repeatedly emphasized compliance, regulatory connectivity, filing/pilot prospects, zero overdue, bank depository, risk resistance, and “safe and trustworthy” operation. A lender belief that “other P2P platforms will be eliminated, but Yooli may be one of the compliant survivors” was therefore understandable in the information environment of the time.

That shifts the investigation from “why did investors fail to leave?” toward:

> **when did the platform know about underlying default, compensation pressure, and worsening claim-transfer liquidity, and were those changes disclosed timely, completely, and in a form ordinary lenders could understand?**

## 12. Evidence-use notes

If this timeline is later used in litigation, police reporting, petitions, or public publication, three categories of wording should remain distinct:

- **“Yooli's announcement said / website displayed”** — the platform's own public statement;
- **“media citing National Internet Finance Association or platform data reported”** — secondary source useful for corroboration;
- **“lenders complained/reported”** — establishes that the complaint existed, not that its substantive allegations were proven.

Two overstatements should be especially avoided.

First, **first-batch information-disclosure pilots, product-registration pilots, and real-time data reporting must not be rewritten as “Yooli completed P2P filing” or “the government formally approved Yooli as a regulatory-pilot institution.”** Yooli's own filing page continued showing the local financial regulator, filing date, and filing number as “to be disclosed after filing.”

Second, **“zero overdue + existing compensation” must not automatically be characterized as falsified data.** Under the applicable disclosure definition, timely third-party compensation can keep lender-facing overdue at zero. The more important issues are the compensating parties, their capacity, the true underlying borrower-default situation, and whether the platform's overall disclosure adequately communicated the accumulation of risk.

Likewise, allegations involving **retained repayments, a fund pool, asset transfer, or self-financing** should remain investigative questions unless supported by judicial findings, complete cash-flow records, look-through audit evidence, or regulatory conclusions.
