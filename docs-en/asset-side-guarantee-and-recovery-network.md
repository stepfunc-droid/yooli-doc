# Yooli Asset-Side Guarantee and Recovery Network Investigation

> Updated: 2026-10-16  
> Chinese original: [`有利网资产端担保与追偿网络调查.md`](../docs/有利网资产端担保与追偿网络调查.md)  
> This article follows *Yooli Asset-Side and Related-Company Structure*, *Yooli Asset-Side Company Look-Through Investigation*, and *Yooli Asset-Side Debt-Assignment Clues*. It focuses on the **guarantee, compensation, claim-assignment, and judicial-recovery network** behind Yooli's asset side.
>
> **Evidentiary boundary:** the fact that a guarantee company later sued a borrower does not mean the recovered money necessarily “should have gone back to Yooli lenders.” If the guarantor had already compensated the lender, the recovery right would ordinarily transfer to the guarantor by law or contract. The more important question is whether **claims belonging to lenders who had not been compensated or paid out were transferred away, and whether the transfer consideration and later recoveries were transparent.**
>
> This English version is a research translation. If wording differs, the Chinese original controls.

## 1. Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee: an important fixed guarantor in the Puhui Kuaixin consumer-loan chain

Further review of court decisions shows that Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. was more central than an ordinary peripheral partner.

### 1.1 Chengxixiang appears as a fixed guarantor in Yooli consumer-finance multi-party contracts

A Shandong court's published representative case, *Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. v. Wang Yongchao*, reconstructs the legal structure:

- the borrower had a loan-service / mandate relationship with **Shenzhen Puhui Kuaixin Financial Services Co., Ltd.**;
- the borrower entered the platform loan relationship through **Beijing Honghe Boji Financial Information Service Co., Ltd. (Yooli)**;
- **Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd.** provided joint and several guarantee liability for the Yooli loan;
- the borrower separately had a sale-of-goods relationship with the merchant.

The court emphasized that, although the contracts had different forms, they all arose from the same Yooli-platform financing and therefore had to be examined together.

Source:

- Shandong courts representative case, *Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. v. Wang Yongchao*  
  https://www.sdcourt.gov.cn/lymyfy/402927/402917/8927143/2022101709080771047.pdf

### 1.2 A repeated Puhui–Yooli–Chengxixiang chain appears across cases

Public cases repeatedly show:

> consumer purchases phone/computer or other goods  
> → Puhui Kuaixin sources and reviews borrower  
> → Yooli matches funds from registered lenders  
> → Chengxixiang provides joint guarantee  
> → borrower defaults  
> → Honghe Boji issues a Compensation Notice  
> → Chengxixiang compensates the creditor(s)  
> → Chengxixiang obtains a recovery right and sues borrowers nationwide

The Chen Changhe case, for example, reviews the contracts among Yooli, Puhui Kuaixin, and Chengxixiang. The actual commodity principal was RMB 3,698, and the courts separately reviewed the actual principal and various management/service charges.

Source:

- Chen Changhe / Shenzhen Chengxixiang Financing Guarantee recovery dispute  
  [《陈昌和、深圳市诚信祥融资担保有限公司追偿权纠纷民事二审民事判决书》（2020）琼96民终2868号](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/陈昌和、深圳市诚信祥融资担保有限公司追偿权纠纷民事二审民事判决书)

### 1.3 Chengxixiang was, at the relevant time, a licensed financing-guarantee company

Guangdong/Shenzhen regulatory materials show that Chengxixiang held a financing-guarantee business license and passed regulatory annual review. Its contractual guarantee role was therefore not merely a label attached to an ordinary consulting company.

## 2. A major anomaly: in 2020 courts began reopening groups of Chengxixiang recovery judgments and transferring cases to police

The most unusual new clue is not a corporate-registration change but a sudden change in how some courts treated Chengxixiang's historical recovery litigation.

### 2.1 Huai'an cases: prior judgments vacated, suits dismissed, whole matters transferred to public-security authorities

On 2020-04-24, Huai'an Intermediate People's Court in Jiangsu handled a cluster of retrial cases involving Chengxixiang, including:

- (2020) Su 08 Min Zai No. 115 — Zhu Tingting;
- (2020) Su 08 Min Zai No. 116 — Zhao Meng;
- (2020) Su 08 Min Zai No. 117 — Zhang Weiyang;
- (2020) Su 08 Min Zai No. 118 — Yan Zhaowu;
- (2020) Su 08 Min Zai No. 87 — Qian Nanjing.

The common structure was:

1. Huaiyin District Court had previously issued effective civil judgments supporting Chengxixiang's recovery claims;
2. the cases were later reopened;
3. the retrial court stated that **Chengxixiang “may be suspected of a crime”;**
4. the prior civil judgments were vacated;
5. Chengxixiang's suits were dismissed;
6. **the entire matters were transferred to public-security authorities for investigation;**
7. Chengxixiang appealed, arguing that the suspicion-of-crime conclusion was factually wrong;
8. Huai'an Intermediate Court dismissed the appeals and upheld the retrial outcomes.

Public decision indexes:

- https://www.tingsonglaw.com/document?doctypeno=2&fornext=ae173e8abd8879f8a9bbab567957d2b9&p=177
- https://www.tingsonglaw.com/document?doctypeno=2&fornext=e1c450b6fd52bf00871f3e5cb78faa89&p=176

This was not a single isolated mention of possible criminal conduct. It was a concentrated retrial treatment of multiple Chengxixiang recovery cases in the same region.

### 2.2 Other regions also reopened old Chengxixiang cases

In 2020 the Luyi County People's Court in Henan reopened the effective judgment in Chengxixiang v. Liu Xuechao:

- original case: (2019) Yu 1628 Min Chu No. 1334;
- retrial review: (2020) Yu 1628 Min Jian No. 31.

The public ruling states that, after the court president submitted the matter to the adjudication committee, the prior effective judgment was considered to contain “indeed erroneous” elements and was ordered retried.

Source:

- https://item.wusiku.top/A/fn/5b/je/uk.htm

Other public judicial information also indicates Chengxixiang recovery cases entering retrial procedures in Anyang, Nanjing, and elsewhere.

### 2.3 The most important missing fact: why did the courts say Chengxixiang “may be suspected of a crime”?

The publicly available Huai'an retrial decisions record the procedural outcome but do not identify the suspected offense, the specific criminal facts, or the later police result.

Therefore, the evidence **does not support saying that these were “Yooli criminal cases,” nor that Chengxixiang was proven to have committed a crime.**

No police acceptance/filing document, criminal judgment, or sufficiently complete record identifying the alleged criminal conduct has yet been located for those borrowers. The project therefore does not attach labels such as “routine loan fraud,” “false litigation,” or “illegal business operation” to these cases.

But the cluster remains highly important because:

- other judgments establish Chengxixiang as a guarantor in the Yooli/Puhui consumer-loan chain;
- it filed recovery actions nationwide;
- by 2019–2020 some courts began reopening previously effective recovery judgments;
- at least the Huai'an cluster was expressly transferred to police because the court said Chengxixiang might be implicated in criminal conduct.

The key next question is **what criminal clue was transferred, and whether the underlying loans in those retrials also came from the Puhui Kuaixin–Yooli system.**

## 4. Guoyin Shengda: another key guarantor under capital and regulatory pressure around 2020

Shenzhen Guoyin Shengda Financing Guarantee Co., Ltd. appears repeatedly in cases involving Puhui Kuaixin, Yooli, and Litong auto loans.

### 4.1 Registered capital reduction from RMB 700 million to RMB 300 million in February 2020

On 2020-02-21, the Shenzhen local financial regulator formally approved Guoyin Shengda's reduction of registered capital from **RMB 700 million to RMB 300 million**.

Source:

- Shenzhen Local Financial Supervision and Administration Bureau  
  https://jr.sz.gov.cn/sjrb/xxgk/tzgg/content/post_6926024.html

The reduction occurred only months before Yooli's mid-2020 repayment crisis became public. Current evidence does not establish direct causation.

### 4.2 Regulatory status deteriorated further

In March 2021, Shenzhen regulators publicly warned that:

- Guoyin Shengda's financing-guarantee license had been valid through **2020-09-17**;
- actual controller Li Baoxiang had been listed by courts as a **dishonest judgment debtor and subject to consumption restrictions**;
- institutions and individuals should conduct business with caution.

Source:

- https://jr.sz.gov.cn/sjrb/xxgk/tzgg/content/post_8644310.html

At minimum, the timeline shows that part of Yooli's historical external compensation infrastructure was itself under material pressure around the time the platform's lender-side crisis became public.

This supports an investigation hypothesis: **a guarantee/compensation system that had previously absorbed borrower default outside the lender-facing layer may have been losing the ability to continue doing so.** It remains a hypothesis, not a proven single causal chain.

## 5. Zhangduoduo Investment: a secondary assignee / collector of guarantor recovery claims

Following the Guoyin Shengda recovery chain leads to **Zhangduoduo Investment (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd.**

### 5.1 Zhangduoduo reportedly acquired Yooli-related recovery claims from Guoyin Shengda

A published case compilation describes *Zhangduoduo Investment (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd. v. Lai [redacted]* as follows:

- Guoyin Shengda first compensated obligations arising from the borrower's Yooli-related debt;
- Zhangduoduo claimed that it acquired Guoyin Shengda's recovery right through **claim assignment**;
- the court therefore reviewed both the underlying Yooli loan and the later assignment.

The compilation also provides a useful breakdown of the loan:

- nominal loan principal: **RMB 145,000**;
- borrower actually received: **RMB 100,000**;
- Zhangduoduo explained the **RMB 45,000** difference as a “Lixin service fee”;
- the court reportedly found insufficient evidence of corresponding services and treated the charge as a financing cost rather than actual principal.

RMB 45,000 was approximately **31.03%** of the nominal principal.

Sources (secondary case compilations; original judgment not yet located):

- https://www.sohu.com/a/793441053_121795084
- https://www.sohu.com/a/865934724_121123868

Because the original judgment has not yet been obtained, these figures should remain marked **pending verification against the primary decision**. They nevertheless point to an important issue: some Yooli-linked assets may have had nominal principal materially above the amount actually received by the borrower because of asset-side service charges.

### 5.2 Zhangduoduo was still bringing recovery-right litigation in 2026

Public hearing information shows Zhangduoduo still involved in recovery litigation in 2026, including:

- (2026) Hu 0115 Min Chu No. 86401;
- Shanghai Pudong New Area People's Court;
- scheduled hearing 2026-10-21.

Public corporate information also shows registered capital of RMB 100 million and zero insured employees.

Source:

- https://www.sohu.com/a/1078882160_122014422

There is **no hard evidence that Zhangduoduo is the anonymous “third-party AMC” mentioned in Yooli's 2021–2022 wind-down notices**. The two must not be conflated. Current evidence supports only that Zhangduoduo acted as an assignee/litigation holder of some Yooli-related guarantor recovery rights.

## 6. Asset-side charges: nominal “loan principal” could materially exceed the amount the borrower actually received

Multiple decisions reveal a recurring issue: **asset-side service, management, or credit-enhancement fees were sometimes deducted from the nominal loan amount at disbursement.**

That means the “principal” shown to a lender or later shown as a nominal bad-debt balance may not equal the cash or commodity value actually obtained by the borrower.

### Example 1: Pei Haoqi — phone RMB 3,690, nominal platform principal RMB 4,428

A Peking University Financial Law Research Center empirical study of **4,531 judgments** cited a Yooli–Puhui–Chengxixiang case as a representative intermediary-fee dispute.

In Dengfeng People's Court **(2018) Yu 0185 Min Chu No. 5149**:

- phone price: **RMB 3,690**;
- nominal principal under Yooli loan agreement: **RMB 4,428**;
- loan-management fee: **RMB 738**;
- fee = **20%** of actual commodity price;
- after compensation, Chengxixiang sought **RMB 6,724.66** plus capital-occupation interest.

The court treated the RMB 738 as a high borrowing cost charged through an intermediary arrangement and did not count it as actual principal.

Source:

- Peking University Financial Law Research Center, empirical study of online-loan intermediary fees  
  https://www.finlaw.pku.edu.cn/jrfy/gk/2020/2020zd104j/37finlaw521844.htm

### Example 2: Chen Changhe — goods RMB 3,698, nominal principal RMB 5,178

Hainan No. 1 Intermediate People's Court, **(2020) Qiong 96 Min Zhong No. 2868**, disclosed the fee structure in detail:

- mobile-phone price / requested financing amount: **RMB 3,698**;
- nominal Yooli loan principal: **RMB 5,178**;
- “loan management fee” deducted at disbursement: **RMB 1,480**;
- third-party payment institution transferred only **RMB 3,698** to the merchant;
- Honghe Boji later sent a compensation notice to Chengxixiang, and Chengxixiang paid **RMB 6,603.38** based on nominal principal plus interest and late charges.

The court did not simply accept RMB 5,178 as actual principal. It used the RMB 3,698 actual principal and reviewed interest, management fees, and default charges against the legal interest ceiling.

Source:

- [《陈昌和、深圳市诚信祥融资担保有限公司追偿权纠纷民事二审民事判决书》（2020）琼96民终2868号](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/陈昌和、深圳市诚信祥融资担保有限公司追偿权纠纷民事二审民事判决书)

The funding path was therefore:

> lenders form a **RMB 5,178** nominal claim  
> → Honghe Boji directs third-party payment to deduct **RMB 1,480 management fee**  
> → merchant receives **RMB 3,698**  
> → after default, guarantor compensation notice is initially calculated from the platform's nominal balance.

The lender's nominal principal, the borrower's actual economic benefit, and the asset-side/service parties' upfront fee income could therefore be three different numbers.

### Example 3: Lai / Zhangduoduo — nominal RMB 145,000, actual RMB 100,000

The currently available secondary case compilations report:

- nominal principal: **RMB 145,000**;
- actual amount received: **RMB 100,000**;
- difference: **RMB 45,000**, characterized by Zhangduoduo as a Lixin service fee;
- court reportedly did not accept the RMB 45,000 as principal because corresponding services were not sufficiently proven.

The original judgment remains to be verified, but the structure is consistent with other documented cases.

### Why this matters for the wind-down

This is not only a consumer-protection question; it can affect asset valuation directly:

1. historical platform lending totals may be based on contractual nominal principal rather than actual borrower proceeds;
2. system-reported remaining principal after default may include charges that a court would not fully enforce as principal;
3. guarantor compensation may initially be calculated on platform-contract numbers and later reduced in recovery litigation;
4. a claim with RMB 1 of book principal may therefore have judicially recoverable value materially below RMB 1 even before collection costs and borrower insolvency;
5. an asset-side or service institution may already have received part of the economics as cash at origination.

Individual cases cannot be extrapolated into a platform-wide average. But the structure is no longer a single isolated example and warrants systematic sampling by asset channel.

## 7. Licensed / annual-review-passed status and court referral for suspected crime can coexist

A potentially confusing sequence involving Chengxixiang must be stated carefully:

- **2020-01-14:** Shenzhen regulators renewed Chengxixiang's financing-guarantee business license, No. Yue (Shenzhen) A0006;
- **2020-04-24:** Huai'an Intermediate Court upheld retrial rulings transferring a cluster of recovery cases to police because Chengxixiang “may be suspected of a crime”;
- **2021-02-26:** Shenzhen regulators published the 2019 financing-guarantee annual-review results, with Chengxixiang marked **“annual review passed.”**

Sources:

- license renewal  
  https://jr.sz.gov.cn/gkmlpt/content/6/6926/post_6926944.html
- 2019 annual review  
  https://jr.sz.gov.cn/sjrb/ydmh/isz/wgk/content/post_8691867.html
- Huai'an retrial case  
  https://www.tingsonglaw.com/document?doctypeno=2&fornext=ae173e8abd8879f8a9bbab567957d2b9&p=177

These facts do not logically contradict one another. Administrative licensing and annual review primarily concern whether an enterprise satisfies regulatory operating requirements, while the court dealt with whether **particular transactions and particular recovery cases may involve suspected criminal conduct**.

The correct conclusion is therefore neither “regulators approved it, so the court must have been wrong” nor “the court transferred cases, so regulators must have covered up wrongdoing.” The record shows that **around 2020 Chengxixiang could remain administratively licensed while particular transactions were viewed with serious suspicion by individual courts.**

The only way to resolve the Huai'an issue is to obtain the police investigation result or the underlying retrial case file identifying the suspected criminal clue.

## 8. Three highest-priority questions now

### 8.1 What exactly was transferred to police in the Chengxixiang retrial cluster?

Important questions include:

- were the reopened loans all Yooli/Puhui Kuaixin projects;
- why did Huaiyin District Court reopen the judgments after they had become effective;
- did the suspected conduct concern Chengxixiang alone or a wider online-lending transaction chain;
- did police accept or formally file cases, and with what result;
- did similar retrial-and-transfer treatment spread to other regions?

### 8.2 How common and how large were upfront asset-side charges?

If these were standardized product structures rather than isolated cases, they could explain a significant economic distinction between **lender-visible principal, the amount actually received by borrowers, and revenue already collected by asset-side/service institutions.**

### 8.3 How much could the “full principal-and-interest protection” partners actually perform in 2020?

Historical Dingcunbao agreements defined guarantee arrangements broadly, including guarantees, mortgages, pledges, compensation, claim repurchase, backup loans, and other forms of full principal-and-interest protection. Later risk disclosures explicitly listed “guarantee company unable to compensate” as a risk.

Sources:

- early Dingcunbao agreement  
  https://www.yooli.com/static/laws/dcbxyStatic.jsp
- later Dingcunbao agreement  
  https://www.yooli.com/static/laws/dcbxyStaticV3.jsp
- risk disclosure  
  https://www.yooli.com/queryAgreeContentForRiskStatic.action?riskType=0

The data most likely to explain why risk suddenly reached lenders in 2020 may therefore be not merely “bad debt increased,” but **when the actual compensation capacity and payment volumes of Chengxixiang, Guoyin Shengda, and other partners began to weaken.**

That line is more likely to explain the severe loss in asset value than further speculation about whether a deregistered shell company happened to retain cash.
